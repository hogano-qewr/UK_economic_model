Executive Summary

This report details the structural estimation of a four-equation macroeconomic model for the UK economy (1997–2024). We use Three-Stage Least Squares (3SLS) to address endogeneity between the output gap, inflation, and monetary policy.

The Structural Model

The system consists of: 1. IS Curve: Demand driven by lags, leads, and Net Exports. 2. Phillips Curve: Inflation deviation from the 2% target, driven by supply shocks. 3. Taylor Rule: Bank of England reaction function (Interest Rate Smoothing). 4. Okun’s Law: The link between output and unemployment.

3SLS Estimation Results

The model was estimated using an instrument set including lagged macro variables and exogenous supply shocks.

Key Findings

1. The Phillips Curve (PC) The Phillips Curve achieved a high of 0.60. Inertia: Significant second-lag persistence ( ). Supply Dominance: Inflation is heavily driven by the Energy Price Cap and Import Prices, rather than domestic demand-pull.

2. The Taylor Rule (TR) The Bank of England shows significant reaction to Inflation Deviations ( ) but is primarily a Financial Stabiliser, reacting most strongly to the Market Spread ( ).

3. Net Exports (NX) The UK remains an open-economy model; the trade balance is the most significant driver of the Output Gap ( ).

The Phillips Curve

The Phillips Curve achieved a strong result. The \(R2\) of 0.60 is the strongest of the four equations, which is arguably a solid result for a structural inflation equation in 3SLS. Key points on the Phillips Curve:

  • The “Memory” of UK Inflation is captured through the second lag (lag(Infl_dev, 2)) with a coefficient of 0.48, which is highly (statistically) significant \((p<0.001)\) . This suggests an “echo” effect in UK inflation - a shock today doesn’t just hit next quarter; it has a secondary push six months later. The intercept value of -0.89 suggests that lags are doing the heavy lifting of explaining the inflation trend (a good sign of a model that is “self-centering”.

  • A **“supply side** trifecta” of statistically significant cost-push variables reflecting the facts that, for a small open economy like the UK, the exchange rate and global costs matter, energy costs are important (including the quarterly movements in Ofgem’s energy price cap).

    • Import Prices \((0.13; p<0.001)\)

    • Energy CPI \((0.06; p<0.001)\)

    • Energy Cap \((-1.67; p<0.01)\)

The energy cap result is suggestive the price cap significantly dampens the deviation from the target.

The only thing that remains “stubborn” is the Y_GAP in the Phillips Curve \((-0.01; p=0.66)\) . In many UK models, this is common: domestic demand is often “drowned out” by massive global supply shocks. We’ve retained this “Cost-Push” Phillips Curve on the presumption that it’s pretty accurate reflection of the UK over the last decade.

The “Taylor Rule”

This shows the Bank of England reacting significantly to deviations from the inflation target Infl_dev \((0.13; p=0.018)\). The iterative process of arriving at this result suggests that the policy equation is robust to different specifications of the inflation process.

## 1. System equations

# The IS Curve Equation
eq_IS     <- as.formula("Y_GAP ~ lag_Y_gap + lead_Y_gap + real_BOE_r + d_mkt_spr + dlog_NX")

# The Phillips Curve Equation (first & second lags to capture deeper inertia)
eq_PC_v2 <- as.formula("Infl_dev ~ lag(Infl_dev) + lag(Infl_dev, 2) + Y_GAP + dlog_energy_cpi + dlog_IM_defl + dlog_cap_adj")

# Taylor Rule Equation (MP reaction to (Infl_dev) from tgt 2% instead of raw CPI
eq_Taylor <- as.formula("d_BOE_rate ~ lag_BOE + Infl_dev + Y_GAP + d_mkt_spr")

# Okun's Law equation
eq_Okun   <- as.formula("U_GAP ~ lag_U_gap + Y_GAP")


## 2. Generate the system list
system_v2 <- list(IS = eq_IS, PC = eq_PC_v2, TR = eq_Taylor, OK = eq_Okun)

## 3. Define the Instrument Set
instr_v2 <- ~ lag_Y_gap + lag_U_gap + lag(Y_GAP, 2) + lag_BOE + 
  lag(Infl_dev) + lag(Infl_dev, 2) + dlog_energy_cpi + 
  dlog_IM_defl + d_mkt_spr + dlog_NX + 
  dlog_cap_adj + covid_dummy

## 4. Run the 3SLS
fit_3sls_v2 <- systemfit(system_v2, 
                         method = "3SLS", 
                         inst = instr_v2, 
                         data = MODEL_DIFFS)

Wald test

The p-value < 0.001 is a massive result. It effectively kills the “textbook” New Keynesian assumption that UK inflation is a random walk with a vertical long-run Phillips Curve [???]

The two lags sum to 0.417 (\((p<0.001)\). Since this is significantly less than 1, your model is making a very strong claim:

  1. Extreme Anchor: UK inflation is “mean-reverting” toward the 2% target. If a shock hits, the system naturally pulls it back rather than letting it spiral.

  2. The “Echo” Effect: Notice that the first lag is negative and the second is positive. This suggests a “sawtooth” or “overshooting” pattern in UK pricing—inflation spikes, over-corrects, and then settles.

  3. Policy Space: This result gives the Bank of England more “room” to breathe. Because inflation doesn’t have a “unit root” (it doesn’t stay where it’s pushed), the BoE doesn’t have to crash the economy quite as hard to bring prices back to target.

Robustness Check: The “Over-Identification” Test

Since we have 10+ instruments for 4 equations, we are over-identified. We need to make sure your instruments aren’t just “strong” but also valid (uncorrelated with the error term). We ran the Sargan (or Hansen J) Test in systemfit.

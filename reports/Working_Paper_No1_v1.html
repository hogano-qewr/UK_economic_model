1 Introduction [DRAFTING NOTES]

Aademic macroeconomists produce models of the economy that typically adhere strictly to the prescriptions of their microeconomic foundations - the laws of motion of the economy are governed by the optimising behaviour of agents operating within it. These models are primarily concerned with the assessment of policy, and often, the appraisal of what constitutes optimal policy. The policy prescriptions that follow from such an approach depend on the internal consistency of the models used and the structural stability of those models in the face of changes to policy regimes.

Large-scale macroeconomic models are a different class and are primarily concerned with forecasting. These models are not usually derived from microeconomic foundations and rely more heavily upon a good fit with the data. Such models are vulnerable to the Lucas critique because the behavioural relationships observed in the data cannot be considered structural and could change when the operation of policy changes. While this is a problem for policy analysts, it is less so for forecasters, who are not typically asked to forecast the effect of significant changes to the macroeconomic policy framework.

An example is the OBR’s main macroeconomic model, which allows a forecaster to apply judgement in a structured and consistent way. The fact that judgement is necessary in forecasting is undisputed and must form an important feature of the approach taken to a model for forecasting. The size and scope of OBR’s main model are important for generating forecasts of the numerous variables required to undertake rigorous fiscal forecasts.

The model presented here is more akin to Murrary’s small model of the UK economy. Its intended use is to provide quick and easily-understood forecasts of core variables and the relatively simple assessment of alternative economic scenarios.

Murrary (2012) notes that, given the trade-off between models that replicate the inertial responses of output, inflation and interest rates and those which most closely adhere to the forward-looking behaviour expected of agents in the economy, it stands to reason that the model with the greatest practical flexibility might be one which draws from both of the classes described above. This issue is described at length in Pagan (2003).

The model presented here sits broadly between the two classes of model described above - borrowing the more desirable features of micro-founded models but relaxing some of the stricter assumptions to allow a better fit with the data.

While larger models permit a wider assessment of the effects of different types of shock, a smaller model need not imply a less accurate forecast. Small size can be a strength; making it easier to trace the transmission of shocks through the model onto key variables, and easier for the modeller to experiment with alternative assumptions, such as the degree of forward-looking behaviour that agents exhibit.

2 A structural macro-econometric model of the UK economy

The model is a hybrid New Keynesian system, defined by the following system of seven stochastic equations, following the semi-structural traditions of OBR(2012) where variables are defined in gap form (tilde) as deviations from their respective trends (using HP filters). The exceptions are net exports and the real exchange rate, the stationarity of which is guaranteed through the use of log-differences, represented by \(\Delta\). Our contemporaneous inflation variable \(\tilde{\pi}_t\) is the deviation of the annualised change in the quaterly GDP deflator (our proxy for domestic inflation) from the Bank of England’s target. This is 2% on the basis of CPI, but higher for the GDP deflator (which we set at 2.25%).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{IS (agg dem):} \quad & \tilde{Y}_t = \alpha_1 \tilde{Y}_{t-1} - \phi_r \tilde{r}_t - \phi_{i^{spr}} \tilde{i}^{spr}_t + \alpha_2 \Delta \text{NX}_t + \alpha_3 \Delta \text{RER}_{t-1} + \epsilon_t^{\text{IS}} \\ \text{PC (inflation):} \quad & \tilde{\pi}_t = \beta_1 \tilde{\pi}_{t-1} + \beta_2 \tilde{\pi}_{t-2} + \kappa_Y \tilde{Y}_t + \beta_3 \tilde{\pi}_t^{\text{IM}} + \beta_4 \tilde{\pi}_t^{\text{E}} + \beta_5 \text{BP}_t^{\pi} + \epsilon_t^{\text{PC}} \\ \text{TR (mon pol):} \quad & i_t = \rho i_{t-1} + (1-\rho)(r^* + \pi^* + \tau_{\pi} \tilde{\pi}_t + \tau_Y \tilde{Y}_t) + \epsilon_t^{\text{TR}} \\ \text{OLU (lab dem):} \quad & \tilde{U}_t = \delta_1 \tilde{U}_{t-1} - \delta_2 \tilde{Y}_t + \delta_3 \text{BP}_t^{U1} + \delta_4 \text{BP}_t^{U2} + \epsilon_t^{\text{OLU}} \\ \text{WPC (lab sup):} \quad & \tilde{w}_t = \theta_1 \tilde{w}_{t-1} + \lambda_{\pi} \tilde{\pi}_{t-1} + \lambda_U \tilde{U}_t + \theta_2 \tilde{P}_t + \epsilon_t^{\text{WPC}} \\ \text{OLP (produc):} \quad & \tilde{P}_t = \eta_1 \tilde{P}_{t-1} + \eta_2 \tilde{Y}_t + \epsilon_t^{\text{OLP}} \\ \text{UIP (exch rate):} \quad & \Delta \text{RER}_t = \mu_1 \Delta \text{RER}_{t-1} + \psi \tilde{i}_t^{diff} + \mu_2 \Delta \text{NX}_t + \mu_3 \tilde{\pi}_t^{\text{IM}} + \epsilon_t^{\text{UIP}} \end{aligned} \]

3 Data diagnostics

3.1 The data used

We use quarterly data spanning the period 1997-2024. It was not possible to include 2025 as these data were not yet available for some variables. We have sought to minimise degrees of freedom lost to ldata transformation and lagging choices. The minimum loss was six and, as such, the model is able to use 106 observations for each variable, spanning 1998 Q3 to 2024 Q4. The continuous maintenance of the data preparation coding script and the continuous recovery of degrees of freedom lost to further transformations and lagging as we evolved through model development materially improved regime identification and equation behaviour across the system, allowing a clearer separation between persistent structural changes and large transitory shocks.

This annex covers the cleaning and diagnostic testing of the data for each variable, including stationarity and structural‑break testing, de-trending, transformation into gaps or first differences, multicollinearity diagnostics, autocorrelation and lag structure assessment, as well as scaling and economic plausibility checks. In applying these diagnostic procedures to the quarterly macroeconomic time series data prior to model estimation, the objective was to ensure that all endogenous and exogenous variables are appropriately transformed, stationary, free from undue multicollinearity and that they exhibit time‑series properties consistent with their theoretical roles within the system.

3.3.2 Stationarity with Structural Breaks Testing

To ensure robustness of stationarity and to identify potential regime shifts, each series was tested for breaks using Zivot–Andrews one‑break unit‑root tests and Bai–Perron multiple‑break tests.

Structural break tests (Zivot–Andrews and Bai–Perron) and subsequent equation‑level diagnostics motivated our minimal and targeted structural break strategy. Persistent regime shifts are identified only where economically meaningful and empirically robust: a Global Financial Crisis (GFC) break and a post‑2013 repair regime in the unemployment gap, and a single inflation‑regime break dating from early 2020. Breaks are explicitly rejected for output gaps, wages, productivity, exchange rates, and monetary policy, where apparent instability reflects shock volatility rather than changes in underlying structure.

Corresponding break dummies were included only in the equations whose dependent variables exhibited genuine structural breaks, thus seeking parsimony and stability.

4 Equation and System Estimation Strategy

The estimation of the semi‑structural macroeconomic model proceeds in several structured stages.
The goal is to ensure that each behavioural equation is statistically well‑specified, dynamically coherent, and suitable for joint estimation within a system that accounts for cross‑equation relationships and endogeneity. The overall approach follows standard practice in semi‑structural modelling: (i) single‑ equation OLS diagnostics → (ii) limited‑information IV estimation (2SLS) → (iii) full‑information system estimation (3SLS) → (iv) calibration and normalisation.

4.1 Single‑Equation OLS Diagnostics

As a first step, each behavioural equation is estimated individually using ordinary least squares (OLS).
OLS estimates are not used for inference or for constructing the final parameter set. Instead, the OLS stage serves four essential diagnostic purposes:

  1. Sign and magnitude validation Coefficients are checked against theoretical priors (e.g., negative response of unemployment to the output gap; positive inflation persistence).

  2. Dynamic specification validation OLS estimates confirm the appropriate lag structure, consistent with ACF/PACF analysis performed earlier (e.g., AR(1) dynamics in the output and unemployment gaps).

  3. Residual behaviour assessment Breusch–Godfrey tests (for serial correlation), Breusch–Pagan tests (for heteroskedasticity), visual residual diagnostics (plots, ACF, distributions). These tests ensure that residual patterns are consistent with theoretical expectations and that no additional dynamics or break adjustments are needed.

  4. Identification of equations affected by simultaneity The Phillips Curve and Wage Phillips Curve exhibit classic endogeneity patterns (wrong signs on output/unemployment gaps under OLS and significant autocorrelation in residuals). This confirms the necessity of instrumental‑variables estimation in the next stage.

The remaining equations (IS, Okun, Productivity, UIP, and the Taylor Rule) display well‑behaved OLS diagnostics, validating their structural form.

4.2 Limited‑Information Instrumental‑Variables Estimation (2SLS)

OLS estimation is inconsistent when regressors are endogenous. Based on the OLS diagnostic stage, two equations require instrumental‑variables treatment:

  • Price Phillips Curve — output gap is endogenous through joint determination with inflation.

  • Wage Phillips Curve — unemployment is endogenous due to labour‑market simultaneity, and the COVID furlough episode amplifies the bias.

For each of these equations, we estimate Two‑Stage Least Squares (2SLS) models using only the equations of interest (“limited‑information” IV estimation). This involves:

  1. Identifying endogenous regressors: \(\tilde{Y}_t\) in the Price Phillips Curve and \(\tilde{U}_t\) in the Wage Phillips Curve.
  2. Constructing an instrument set from: the lagged values of the gaps, \(\tilde{Y}_{t-2}\) and \(\tilde{Y}_{t-3}\) for output and \(\tilde{U}_{t-2}\) and \(\tilde{U}_{t-3}\) for unemployment.
  3. Estimating each equation separately usingivreg() in R: provides more consistent coefficient estimates, a correction for simultaneity bias and cleaned up residual dynamics.

This stage ensures that, prior to system estimation, the most problematic equations (the Phillips Curves) are already structurally identified.

4.3 Full‑Information System Estimation (3SLS)

With the structural equations validated and simultaneity addressed, we estimate the entire 7‑equation model jointly using Three‑Stage Least Squares (3SLS) via the systemfit() framework in R. Full‑ information estimation is appropriate for this model because:

  1. Equations are linked through shared endogenous variables (e.g. output gap interacts with inflation, real interest rates, unemployment, wages).

  2. Cross‑equation error correlations carry useful information e.g., cost‑push inflation shocks (import prices, energy) often correlate with exchange‑rate and real‑rate shocks.

  3. System estimation improves efficiency 3SLS uses the entire estimated covariance matrix of residuals to weight each equation appropriately.

  4. Structural identification is system‑wide Parameter calibration (e.g., Taylor Rule coefficients, Okun elasticity) can be imposed consistently across the entire system.

  5. The resulting shocks are system-consistent Each equation produces structural errors that should be interpretable as demand shocks, supply shocks, monetary shocks, wage shocks, etc.

3SLS therefore provides joint estimation, improved precision, correction for remaining endogeneity, and a coherent set of system‑consistent shocks for analysis and forecasting.

4.4 Calibration and Normalisation

Following fully unrestricted 3SLS estimation, coefficients are calibrated gradually and in sequential steps, represented by eight different hybrid versions of the original model (Hybrid A-H). Each hybrid is an extension or augmentation of the previous and each is an attempt to extend and strengthen its credibility as a small open economy model over all time horizons.

Calibrated values are standard in semi‑structural models; they improve identification and stabilise system estimation. The hybrid model works because some things are stable and estimable, while others are unstable and should be calibrated. Calibrations include policy‑rule coefficients in the Taylor Rule, small elasticity parameters where identification is weak (e.g., IS curve’s long-term spread term), and steady‑state relationships (e.g., neutral real interest rate, inflation target).

Extensions and augmentations are necessary when we awaken UIP for explicit SOE dynamics, which requires the supply-side and slack to be specified more thoroughly. The parameter sets for each model variant provide the basis for impulse‑response analysis, historical shock decomposition, counterfactual simulations, and model‑based forecasting.

5 Single-equation OLS diagnostics

Single‑equation OLS diagnostics show strong internal coherence across blocks. Real‑side equations (IS, Okun’s law, and productivity) exhibit heteroskedastic but serially uncorrelated residuals, consistent with state‑dependent shock amplitudes during crisis periods but stable adjustment dynamics. Nominal and institutional equations (price Phillips curve, wage Phillips curve, and Taylor rule) display residual serial correlation but fail to reject homoskedasticity, reflecting expected inertia in inflation, wage setting, and policy adjustment rather than misspecification.

Heteroskedasticity‑robust inference sharpens interpretation without altering substantive conclusions. Core structural slopes remain stable across specifications, while marginal significance attached to later crisis dummies dissipates under robust standard errors—correctly distinguishing regime change from shock realisation. In the wage Phillips curve, labour‑market slack affects wage dynamics primarily by slowing mean reversion rather than inducing contemporaneous wage declines, eliminating the need for wage‑specific regime dummies.

Overall, the revised data construction reduces the reliance on ad‑hoc structural adjustments, improves interpretability of break tests, and yields cleaner reduced‑form behaviour across equations. The final specification is parsimonious, economically transparent, and diagnostically stable, providing a sound basis for system estimation and policy analysis.

5.1 The IS relation (aggregate demand)

This equation describes how cyclical output \((\tilde{Y})\) responds to monetary conditions, external demand and exchange‑rate movements.

\[ \text{IS (agg dem):} \quad \tilde{Y}_t = \alpha_1\tilde{Y}_{t-1} - \phi_r\tilde{r}_t - \phi_{i^{spr}}\tilde{i}^{spr}_t + \alpha_2\Delta\text{NX}_t + \alpha_3\Delta\text{RER}_{t-1} + \epsilon_t^{\text{IS}} \]

The lagged output gap term \((\tilde{Y}_{t-1})\) captures the intrinsic persistence of domestic demand, reflecting adjustment costs and slow-moving expectations. The real interest rate gap \((\tilde{r}_t)\) and the credit spread gap \((\tilde{i}^{spr}_t)\) influence output through the standard inter-temporal substitution and financial‑conditions channels: a higher real rate or a wider spread tightens financial conditions and depresses activity. External demand is captured via changes in net exports \((d\text{NX}_t)\), which affect aggregate demand in a small open economy. The lagged real exchange rate \((\Delta\text{RER}_{t-1})\) term captures expenditure‑switching effects, reflecting how movements in relative prices affect competitiveness with a delay.

Together, these elements form a semi‑structural representation of the UK business cycle, linking demand to financial conditions and external shocks. Estimated dynamics include \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \space \text{and} \space \alpha_3\). Following unrestricted 3SLS estimation, we calibrate the demand sensitivities: \(\phi_r, \space \text{and} \space \phi_{i^{spr}}\).

5.2 Phillips curve relation (domestic price inflation)

In this equation, \(\tilde{\pi}_t\) is the inflation deviation from the Bank of England’s target.

\[ \text{PC (inflation):} \quad \tilde{\pi}_t = \beta_1 \tilde{\pi}_{t-1} + \beta_2 \tilde{\pi}_{t-2} + \kappa_Y \tilde{Y}_t + \beta_3 \tilde{\pi}_t^{\text{IM}} + \beta_4 \tilde{\pi}_t^{\text{E}} + \beta_5 \text{BP}_t^{\pi} + \epsilon_t^{\text{PC}} \]

Because we use a domestically-driven measure of inflation (annualised change in the quarterly GDP deflator), we set the target at 2.25% as the base case (equivalent to the 2% target for CPI inflation). \(\tilde{\pi}^\text{IM}_t\) is an import price inflation gap, \(\tilde{\pi}^\text{E}_t\) an energy price inflation gap and \(\text{BP}^{\pi}_t\) is a dummy variable representing the structural break in the inflation regime in 2022 Q1.

The inflation equation links price dynamics to their own persistence, domestic slack, and external cost‑ push forces. The autoregressive terms reflect backward‑looking elements of price setting and the empirical inertia of UK inflation. The output gap enters through the slope parameter, representing the degree to which domestic economic slack influences inflationary pressure. Import price inflation and energy‑price shocks capture external cost‑push channels — key drivers of inflation in an open economy like the UK. The structural‑break dummies account for shifts in inflation dynamics due to major regime changes, such as the global financial crisis and the post‑COVID inflation shock.

This specification results in a hybrid Phillips Curve where domestic cyclical pressure plays a modest role, while external price shocks and persistence dominate short‑run inflation movements. Inflation persistence parameters \((\beta_1, \beta_2)\) are estimated. Import and energy price pass‑through \((\beta_3, \beta_4)\) are also estimated. The Phillips curve slope \((\kappa_Y)\) is later calibrated (at a small but positive value, reflecting the evidence and conclusions of policymakers of a flattening of the Phillips curve in the UK).

5.3 Taylor rule (monetary policy)

The Taylor Rule establishes the central bank reaction function, describing how it sets the policy interest rate in response to economic conditions.

\[ \text{TR (mon pol):} \quad i_t = \rho i_{t-1} + (1-\rho)(r^* + \pi^* + \tau_{\pi} \tilde{\pi}_t + \tau_Y \tilde{Y}_t) + \epsilon_t^{\text{TR}} \]

Policy inertia is captured by the coefficient \((\rho)\) on the lagged nominal policy rate, reflecting gradualism in the Monetary Policy Committee’s decision-making and the desire to avoid unnecessary volatility in financial markets. The central bank reacts to deviations of inflation from its target and to the output gap, with calibrated coefficients \((\tau_{\pi},\tau_Y)\) ensuring determinacy and policy realism. The neutral real interest rate \((r^*)\) and the inflation target \((\pi^*)\) anchor the long‑run level of the policy rate.

This equation captures the systematic component of monetary policy in the UK’s inflation‑targeting regime, in which \(\rho\) (policy inertia) is estimated. For OLS purposes, we estimate the reduced‑form hybrid rule \(i_t = \gamma_0 + \rho i_{t-1} + \gamma_{\pi}\tilde{\pi}_t + \gamma_Y \tilde{Y}_t + \epsilon^{\text{TR}}_t\), in which \(\gamma_{\pi} = (1-\rho)\tau_{\pi}\) and \(\gamma_Y = (1-\rho)\tau_Y\).

5.4 Okun’s Law for Unemployment (labour demand)

The Okun equation links labour‑market slack \((\tilde{U}_t)\) to the business cycle.

\[ \text{OLU (lab dem):} \quad \tilde{U}_t = \delta_1 \tilde{U}_{t-1} - \delta_2 \tilde{Y}_t + \delta_3 \text{BP}_t^{U1} + \delta_4 \text{BP}_t^{U2} + \epsilon_t^{\text{OLU}} \]

The lagged unemployment gap \((\tilde{U}_{t-1})\) reflects persistence in labour‑market conditions, which adjust more slowly than output. 5.5 Wage Phillips curve (labour supply)

The Wage Phillips Curve governs the dynamics of real wage pressure.

\[ \text{WPC (lab sup):} \quad \tilde{w}_t = \theta_1 \tilde{w}_{t-1} + \lambda_{\pi} \tilde{\pi}_{t-1} + \lambda_U \tilde{U}_t + \theta_2 \tilde{P}_t + \epsilon_t^{\text{WPC}} \]

Expected inflation (deviation from target) \([\text{E}_t(\tilde{\pi}_{t+1}) = \tilde{\pi}_{t-1}]\) enters as an exogenous driver of nominal wage demands, ensuring that wage formation remains consistent with long‑run inflation targets. The productivity gap \((\tilde{P}_t)\) term captures how deviations in trend productivity influence wage-setting behaviour, consistent with labour‑cost markups. This equation therefore links wage inflation to both cyclical labour-market conditions and long‑run institutional shifts.

The wage Phillips curve slopes \((\lambda_{\pi}, \lambda_U)\) are the first parameters to be calibrated to establish discipline at the most fundmental level. The dynamic persistence and productivity coefficients \((\theta_1, \theta_2)\) are estimated from the data.

5.7 Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP)

The real‑exchange‑rate equation links currency movements \(\Delta \text{RER}_t\) to interest‑rate differentials and external flows.

\[ \text{UIP (exch rate):} \quad \Delta \text{RER}_t = \mu_1 \Delta \text{RER}_{t-1} + \psi \tilde{i}_t^{diff} + \mu_2 \Delta \text{NX}_t + \mu_3 \tilde{\pi}_t^{\text{IM}} + \epsilon_t^{\text{UIP}} \]

The autoregressive term captures the persistent nature of real exchange rate dynamics. The nominal interest‑rate differential gap \(\tilde{i}^{diff}_t\) reflects the uncovered‑interest‑parity (UIP) condition, with \(\psi\) eventually calibrated to reflect its empirical weakness in UK data. \(\tilde{i}^{diff}_t = (i - i^f) - (i - i^f)^{trend}\), in which \(i\) is the official BOE rate and \(i^f\) is a trade-weighted average of the official central bank rates of the UK’s top 5 trading partners (the Euro Area, USA, China, Switzerland and Norway). This is consistent with our approach to the real exchange rate and our proxy for foreign price levels.

Deviations in net exports influence the real exchange rate through trade‑balance adjustments and the demand for domestic versus foreign currency. Import‑price inflation enters to reflect the import‑cost channel, whereby external cost shocks affect relative prices. This equation provides a stable reduced‑form representation of exchange‑rate dynamics that accommodates both UIP and non‑UIP forces.

5.8 April 5th: Fully-free 3SLS vs. 2SLS/OLS

5.8.1 Interpretation of Price and Wage Phillips Curves

5.8.1.1 Price Phillips Curve

In the fully simultaneous 3SLS estimation, the coefficient on the output gap in the price Phillips curve is negative and statistically significant. With the output gap defined as the deviation of output from potential, this implies that—conditional on the rest of the system—periods of above‑potential activity are associated with lower contemporaneous deviations of inflation from trend.

This result should be interpreted with care. In a system in which monetary policy responds endogenously to both inflation and activity, the reduced‑form Phillips‑curve coefficient captures not only the underlying price‑setting behaviour of firms, but also the stabilising response of policy and associated financial‑conditions effects. In particular, positive output gaps are typically associated with a tightening of monetary conditions, which acts to curb subsequent inflationary pressure. When these feedbacks are estimated simultaneously, the partial correlation between inflation deviations and the output gap within the Phillips‑curve equation reflects net system interactions rather than a standalone behavioural elasticity.

Comparing across estimation methods illustrates this point. Ordinary least squares produce a negative and relatively muted output‑gap coefficient, consistent with simultaneity bias arising from the joint determination of output, inflation, and monetary policy. Two‑stage least squares, which instruments the output gap using lagged real and financial variables, increases the magnitude of the coefficient but leaves the sign unchanged, reflecting the fact that demand disturbances in the data are systematically followed by policy stabilisation. Fully‑free 3SLS further reallocates the effect of demand pressure across the IS curve, the Taylor rule, and the Phillips curve, leaving the Phillips‑curve coefficient to reflect a residual reduced‑form relationship once policy feedback is internalised.

For these reasons, the output‑gap coefficient estimated in the fully‑free system should not be interpreted as a direct measure of the structural inflation–activity trade‑off. In Hybrid‑A, the slope of the Phillips curve is therefore calibrated to impose the economically conventional mechanism—whereby excess demand generates upward pressure on inflation—rather than to match the reduced‑form coefficient that emerges from a fully endogenous policy system. The 3SLS results provide an ex post justification for this approach, showing that unconstrained estimation conflates price‑setting behaviour with the systematic response of monetary policy.

5.8.1.2 Wage Phillips Curve

Fully‑free 3SLS estimation of the wage Phillips curve indicates a high degree of persistence in real wage deviations from trend, with the coefficient on the lagged wage gap close to unity. In contrast, contemporaneous effects of labour‑market slack and inflation on wages are weak and statistically insignificant, once endogeneity across the system is taken into account. Short‑run productivity deviations enter with a sizeable coefficient, reflecting cyclical utilisation and compositional effects.

Given the definition of the wage gap as the deviation of real wages from their HP‑filtered trend, these results imply that wage adjustment in the UK occurs primarily through gradual correction of past deviations rather than through rapid responses to contemporaneous labour‑market conditions. Episodes of labour‑market slack are associated with prolonged periods of subdued real wage growth, rather than sharp wage cuts, consistent with the presence of nominal and institutional rigidities in wage‑setting.

Comparison across estimation approaches reinforces this interpretation. OLS and 2SLS estimates suggest at most weak contemporaneous effects of unemployment on wages, and these effects are not robust once the full system of interactions is taken into account. In the 3SLS system, labour‑market slack primarily influences wages indirectly—through persistence and through regime shifts captured in the unemployment and output blocks—rather than as a strong within‑quarter compression mechanism.

This motivates the treatment adopted in Hybrid‑A, where the coefficient on labour‑market slack in the wage Phillips curve is calibrated rather than freely estimated. The calibration ensures a stable and monotonic relationship between slack and wage pressure in long‑run dynamics, while avoiding over‑interpretation of short‑run correlations that are weakly identified in the data. The fully‑free 3SLS results support this approach, indicating that wage dynamics are dominated by inertia and adjustment lags, and that contemporaneous slack effects are structurally secondary.

5.8.1.3 Implications for model use

Taken together, the results from the fully‑free 3SLS system suggest that both price and wage Phillips curves are heavily influenced by endogenous feedbacks, particularly through monetary policy and broader system dynamics. Reduced‑form slope coefficients should therefore be interpreted as reflecting net interactions rather than isolated behavioural relationships. Hybrid‑A calibration choices can be viewed as structural discipline devices that preserve economically meaningful transmission mechanisms while ensuring stability and interpretability of the model for scenario analysis and policy applications.

5.9.9 Summary Assessment

The Hybrid‑A 3SLS specification delivers a stable, interpretable semi‑structural system. 5.10 APRIL 5 - HYBRID B : Conditional Forecasting and Scenario Specification

5.10.1 Overview

Hybrid B represents the conditional forecasting and scenario‑analysis configuration of the model. In this specification, the neutral real interest rate is fixed exogenously and the real policy stance is defined relative to this neutral benchmark. This removes trend‑estimation noise and policy‑reaction endogeneity from the interest‑rate channel, allowing the model to be used to trace the effects of an assumed monetary‑policy stance on output, inflation, wages, and the labour market.

Hybrid B builds directly on the structural discipline established in Hybrid A. The Phillips‑curve relationships retain their calibrated slopes, while the remainder of the system is estimated freely using three‑stage least squares (3SLS). The resulting model is explicitly designed for scenario conditioning and policy simulations rather than for structural parameter identification.

5.10.2 Conditioning on the Neutral Real Rate

In Hybrid B, the neutral real rate of interest is fixed at a constant value and the real policy gap is defined as the deviation of the observed short‑term real interest rate from this neutral benchmark. This treatment abstracts from time‑variation in the neutral rate and re‑interprets the interest‑rate channel as a measure of policy stance rather than of policy behaviour.

This approach mirrors standard institutional practice when conditioning forecasts on market expectations or internally specified policy paths. By construction, monetary‑policy feedback to contemporaneous developments in output and inflation is removed from the transmission channel linking real interest rates to demand.

5.10.3 Output and Demand Dynamics (IS Curve)

Under Hybrid B, the IS curve exhibits a smaller but statistically significant response to the real interest‑rate gap. Output persistence remains strong, while the explanatory power of the IS equation declines relative to Hybrid A.

This reduction in fit is expected and desirable in a conditional framework. When policy is treated as exogenous, the model no longer exploits contemporaneous feedback from activity into policy to explain short‑run fluctuations in output. Instead, the IS curve captures the causal impact of a given policy stance on demand. The resulting coefficient can therefore be interpreted more directly as a policy‑transmission elasticity rather than as a reduced‑form correlation.

Other financial and external demand channels remain weak, consistent with earlier results and with the view that short‑run demand dynamics are primarily governed by internal propagation and policy stance.

5.10.4 Price Phillips Curve

In the price Phillips curve, the contemporaneous output‑gap effect remains imposed as a calibrated structural relationship. Under Hybrid B, inflation dynamics continue to be dominated by persistence, with lagged inflation carrying the bulk of explanatory power. Cost‑push variables, including energy and import prices, play a secondary role, while the inflation‑regime dummy remains non‑binding in the presence of strong inertia.

Importantly, fixing the policy stance prevents the Phillips curve from absorbing policy‑reaction effects. Inflation outcomes now reflect the combined influence of inherited persistence and exogenous demand pressure, rather than the endogenous stabilisation present in the fully‑free system. This makes the inflation block well suited for conditional forecasts and counterfactual demand scenarios.

5.10.5 Wage Phillips Curve

The wage Phillips curve under Hybrid B continues to display very high persistence in real wage deviations from trend, reinforcing the conclusion that wage adjustment is slow and institutionally constrained. The calibrated contemporaneous effect of labour‑market slack ensures that adverse labour conditions exert a stabilising influence on wage pressure over time, without relying on weakly identified short‑run correlations.

As in Hybrid A, productivity deviations play a significant role in explaining wage movements, reflecting cyclical utilisation and compositional effects. Inflation pass‑through to wages remains limited. Overall, wage dynamics under Hybrid B are stable and economically plausible, supporting their use in medium‑term labour‑market projections.

5.10.6 Monetary Policy Block

Although the Taylor‑rule equation remains estimated in the Hybrid B system, its role is conceptually diminished. With the real policy gap defined exogenously, the Taylor rule no longer governs the evolution of demand and inflation within the model. Instead, it becomes an inert block whose dynamics are superseded by the imposed policy stance when the model is used operationally.

The persistence and responsiveness of the estimated Taylor rule remain stable, indicating that the imposed conditioning does not distort the underlying policy reaction function. In practical applications, the Taylor rule can be replaced or overridden by an external policy path without affecting the internal consistency of the model.

5.10.7 Labour Market and Productivity

Labour‑market dynamics under Hybrid B remain robust. Okun’s law continues to display strong persistence and a sizeable response of unemployment to the output gap. Structural regime shifts associated with the post‑financial‑crisis period and the subsequent recovery remain clearly identified and statistically significant.

Productivity dynamics weaken modestly relative to Hybrid A, with output exerting a smaller and less precisely estimated influence. This is again consistent with the conditional nature of Hybrid B: once policy stance is fixed, less cyclical variation is attributed to endogenous utilisation adjustments.

5.10.8 External Sector

The exchange‑rate equation continues to be driven primarily by interest‑rate differentials, with limited roles for trade volumes and import prices. Explanatory power remains low, reflecting the inherent volatility of exchange rates and the stylised nature of uncovered interest parity relationships. Hybrid B does not materially alter the behaviour of the external sector.

5.10.9 Residual Correlations and System Coherence

Residual correlations under Hybrid B remain economically interpretable and broadly similar to those observed under Hybrid A. Output, unemployment, wages, and productivity share common disturbances, while inflation innovations are negatively correlated with real‑side shocks, consistent with stabilising policy effects being imposed rather than estimated.

The determinant of the residual covariance matrix falls further relative to Hybrid A, indicating tighter system coherence once policy stance is conditioned. No evidence emerges of instability or misspecification induced by fixing the neutral real rate.

5.10.10 Summary Assessment

Hybrid B delivers a stable and internally coherent conditional forecasting system. By fixing the neutral real interest rate and treating the policy stance as exogenous, the model shifts cleanly from structural identification to policy‑transmission analysis. The IS curve becomes interpretable as a causal demand channel, while inflation and wage dynamics are governed by persistence and calibrated structural relationships.

Crucially, the transition from Hybrid A to Hybrid B does not disrupt strongly identified behavioural relationships elsewhere in the system. Instead, it clarifies the roles of policy stance, demand, and nominal inertia, making Hybrid B well suited for scenario analysis, forecast conditioning, and counterfactual policy exercises.

10 Annex IV - 2SLS analysis of PC and WPC relations

This stage targets specific equations where regressors are endogenous, namely the Phillips Curve and wage Phillips Curve

Both failed the BG test under OLS, indicating serial correlation due to endogenous regressors. The Y_GAP coefficient in the PC had the wrong sign under OLS, classic sign of simultaneity. The u_GAP coefficient in the WPC had the wrong sign, with unemployment and wage setting jointly determined, a classic OLS failure. OLS residuals remain autocorrelated even after the correct lag structure → the model is correct, but OLS is the wrong estimator. Other equations do not suffer from strong simultaneity. IS, Okun, Productivity, UIP, TR can be left to later in the system stage.

2SLS is used to repair the demand/supply blocks before we combine all equations in a 3SLS estimator. This involves instrumenting the endogenous variables using relevant instruments drawn from the full data set (e.g., lags of exogenous variables, predetermined variables, etc.):

  • in the Phillips curve, the endogenous regressors include the output gap \(\tilde{Y}_t\) and possibly the import price inflation gap \(\tilde{\pi}^{\text{IM}}_t\), even if less prominent. The (adaptive) expectations proxy \([\text{E}_t(\tilde{\pi}_{t+1}) = \tilde{\pi}_{t-1}]\) is pre-determined;

  • in the Wage Phillips Curve, the endogenous regressors are the unemployment gap \((\tilde{U}_t)\) and possibly \(\tilde{\pi}_{t-1}\). The lagged wage gap \((\tilde{w}_{t-1})\) is predetermined.

11 Annex V - 3SLS system estimation

Up to now, we have engaged with OLS for diagnostics and structure checking and 2SLS for fixing local endogeneity equation‑by‑equation. Now, with 3SLS:, we estimate all equations jointly, allow cross‑equation error correlation, impose system‑wide identification, stop asking “is this regressor exogenous here?” and start asking “is this variable exogenous in the system?”

This is crucial because the model is explicitly simultaneous: output ↔︎ inflation ↔︎ policy ↔︎ real rates; output ↔︎ unemployment ↔︎ wages ↔︎ inflation; inflation ↔︎ exchange rate ↔︎ import prices. 3SLS is the first estimator that actually respects this structure. Mechanically, 3SLS combines 2SLS logic (endogenous RHS variables are instrumented) and SUR logic (residuals allowed to be correlated across equations). So it answers: “Using only exogenous variation, and accounting for cross‑equation covariance, what is the system‑consistent set of parameters?”

That’s why it is the final estimator.

Once both PC and WPC are fixed via 2SLS, you estimate the whole 7‑equation system jointly, allowing:

  • cross‑equation error covariances

  • structural identification

  • system‑consistent parameters

  • higher efficiency

  • and correction of any remaining endogeneity

You can assemble them into a system of equations and estimate them using 3SLS:

  • ✔ correcting remaining endogeneity

  • ✔ exploiting cross-equation error correlations

  • ✔ obtaining system‑consistent shocks

  • ✔ improving efficiency

  • ✔ producing your final structural parameter set

“Fully free 3SLS” (the first 3SLS run) answers one question only: “If I let the data speak as freely as possible, once simultaneity and cross‑equation correlations are handled, what do the structural relationships want to look like?” This step is analog‑diagnostic, not policy‑ready. It is used to check signs across the whole system, gauge magnitudes relative to priors, see which slopes are weakly identified, see which parameters are trying to do too much work, identify trade‑offs (e.g. PC slope vs policy response); confirm that your structure is coherent once all feedback loops are closed.

At this stage: you do not over‑interpret significance; you expect some coefficients to be noisy; you expect some slopes to be implausible in magnitude. That’s normal — and informative.

Why you don’t start with calibration: If you calibrated before seeing free 3SLS results, you would not know whether: the calibration is supporting the data, or the calibration is suppressing a deeper misspecification. Free 3SLS acts like an X‑ray: it shows where the bones are weak before you put on the cast.

What the hybrid (calibrated) model is doing: once you’ve seen the unrestricted 3SLS results, you then ask: “Which parameters do I actually want the data to identify, and which should be disciplined by theory or external evidence?” Typical examples (very familiar from central‑bank models):

  • Phillips‑curve output slope (κᵧ): often weakly identified → calibrated or tightly constrained

  • Wage Phillips Curve unemployment slope (λᵤ): small and noisy in data → calibrated

  • IS curve semi‑elasticities of long rates: calibrated for stability

  • Policy reaction coefficients: either calibrated or gently constrained

  • Trend / steady‑state parameters: almost always calibrated

The hybrid model therefore: reduces variance where the data are weak, improves numerical stability, prevents trade‑offs between poorly identified slopes, makes counterfactuals interpretable, anchors the model to theory and institutional knowledge. Crucially: You are not calibrating to “improve fit” — you are calibrating to resolve identification problems revealed by free estimation.

We began by estimating the unrestricted system via 3SLS to assess identification and sign consistency. Given weak identification of some structural slopes, we then impose calibrated values for selected parameters in line with standard macroeconomic priors, re‑estimating the remaining parameters using a constrained system estimator.

What we have now is not a collection of regressions, but a system-consistent empirical macro model.

12 Annex VI - System Estimation Results: Fully Unrestricted 3SLS

This section documents the results from estimating the full semi‑structural model using three‑stage least squares (3SLS). The purpose of this stage is diagnostic and informational rather than final: the unrestricted system estimation allows the data to determine parameter values once simultaneity across equations and cross‑equation error correlations are fully accounted for.

12.1 6.1 Rationale for System Estimation

Single‑equation estimation, even with instrumental variables, cannot fully capture the simultaneous feedback mechanisms embedded in the model. Output, inflation, wages, unemployment, monetary policy, productivity, and the exchange rate are jointly determined, and shocks affecting one block of the model propagate through the others contemporaneously.

The 3SLS estimator is therefore employed to:

  • correct for endogeneity across equations,

  • exploit cross‑equation correlations in the error structure, and

  • obtain system‑consistent parameter estimates.

This step follows extensive single‑equation diagnostics and 2SLS estimation for the Phillips Curve and Wage Phillips Curve, where endogeneity was shown to be material.

12.2 6.2 Identification and Instrument Strategy

A lean system‑wide instrument set was constructed to ensure full‑rank identification of the stacked system. Instruments were restricted to predetermined variables and structural break indicators that are plausibly exogenous to contemporaneous shocks. Rank diagnostics confirmed that the resulting instrument matrix is full rank, ensuring numerical stability of the 3SLS estimator.

The guiding principle in designing the instrument set was parsimony rather than maximal coverage: the system estimator borrows identifying variation across equations, so redundant instruments can undermine identification rather than improve it.

12.3 6.3 System Fit and Cross‑Equation Error Structure

At the system level, the unrestricted 3SLS estimation achieves a high degree of explanatory power once cross‑equation correlations are accounted for. While the aggregated OLS measure of fit is moderate, the McElroy system R² indicates that the joint model explains the bulk of variation across the endogenous variables when estimated as a system.

The estimated residual covariance and correlation matrices exhibit substantial off‑diagonal elements, particularly among:

  • output, unemployment, and wage equations;

  • output and productivity;

  • monetary policy and exchange‑rate dynamics.

These correlations confirm the presence of strong common shocks across blocks of the model and provide a compelling justification for system estimation. The magnitudes and signs of these correlations align with major macroeconomic episodes in the sample, including the Global Financial Crisis, the COVID period, and the post‑2021 inflation surge.

12.4 6.4 Core Behavioural Blocks

12.4.1 Output (IS Curve)

The IS equation displays strong internal consistency under 3SLS. Output dynamics are highly persistent, and the real interest rate gap emerges as the dominant cyclical driver of demand. Structural break dummies associated with the COVID recession and the post‑2021 period are large and significant, reflecting substantial regime shifts in activity.

Notably, the coefficient on the real interest rate gap is positive in the unrestricted system. At this stage, this should not be interpreted as a structural demand elasticity. Rather, it reflects the strong endogeneity of monetary policy: periods of strong activity coincide with aggressive policy tightening, and 3SLS correctly attributes this correlation to system‑wide interaction rather than causal stimulus.

12.4.2 Inflation (Phillips Curve)

Inflation exhibits substantial persistence, with lagged inflation terms contributing strongly to current inflation dynamics. Energy and import price shocks enter with the expected positive sign, confirming the importance of cost‑push factors in recent UK inflation episodes.

The output gap enters the unrestricted Phillips Curve with a negative coefficient, even after full system correction for simultaneity. This result indicates that, over the estimation sample, demand effects on inflation operate primarily through the policy reaction function rather than directly via price‑setting behaviour. Inflationary episodes are largely associated with adverse supply shocks coinciding with weak demand conditions.

12.4.3 Wages (Wage Phillips Curve)

Wage dynamics are dominated by strong inertia, with lagged real wage gaps explaining the majority of variation. Inflation expectations play a limited role, and the unemployment gap enters with a small and statistically insignificant coefficient in the unrestricted system.

This finding suggests that labour‑market slack provides only weak discipline over wage growth at quarterly frequencies, particularly in the presence of institutional and policy interventions such as the furlough scheme. Productivity shocks, rather than labour‑market tightness, are the primary driver of deviations in real wages in the sample.

12.4.4 Monetary Policy (Taylor Rule)

The Taylor Rule equation is tightly estimated under 3SLS. Monetary policy exhibits substantial interest‑rate smoothing and responds systematically and significantly to both inflation deviations and the output gap. This equation serves as the stabilising core of the system and largely governs the transmission of demand conditions to other blocks.

12.4.5 Labour Market, Productivity and External Sector

The Okun relationship between output and unemployment is stable and well identified, capturing both persistence in unemployment and the expected negative response to activity. Structural break indicators perform as intended, isolating regime shifts in labour‑market behaviour.

Short‑run productivity dynamics are weakly cyclical and noisy, consistent with measurement error and sectoral composition effects. Exchange‑rate dynamics are volatile and weakly explained by fundamentals, reflecting the well‑documented empirical failure of uncovered interest parity at short horizons.

12.5 6.5 Interpretation of the Unrestricted System

Taken together, the unrestricted 3SLS results characterise an economy in which:

  • inflation dynamics are predominantly supply‑driven;

  • wage formation is highly inertial and weakly responsive to slack;

  • monetary policy plays a central role in mediating demand‑inflation interactions;

  • output fluctuations are largely governed by policy stance rather than autonomous private‑sector demand.

Several behavioural slopes—most notably the Phillips Curve output coefficient and the Wage Phillips Curve unemployment coefficient—are weakly identified in the unrestricted system. This is a familiar feature of modern macroeconomic data and reflects both structural change and the dominance of large non‑demand shocks.

12.6 6.6 Motivation for Hybrid (Calibrated) Estimation

The unrestricted 3SLS results serve their intended purpose: they reveal which parameters the data are able to identify reliably and which are subject to substantial uncertainty or system‑level feedback effects. In particular, demand‑side slopes in price‑ and wage‑setting equations are weakly identified once policy reactions are endogenised.

The next section presents this calibrated system and evaluates whether the imposed discipline improves stability, interpretability, and policy relevance without contradicting the empirical evidence.

13 Annex VII - Hybrid estimation and calibration strategy

The unrestricted 3SLS results serve their intended diagnostic purpose. They identify which parameters are robustly pinned down by the data once simultaneity and cross‑equation feedbacks are fully accounted for, and which parameters are weakly identified or absorb system‑level interactions. In particular, demand‑side slopes in the price‑ and wage‑setting blocks are imprecisely estimated once monetary policy reactions are endogenised, reflecting both the dominance of large supply shocks in the sample and the strong systematic response of policy to macroeconomic conditions.

These findings motivate a hybrid estimation strategy, in which a small subset of behavioural parameters is disciplined using external evidence and theoretical priors, while the remaining parameters are estimated jointly using constrained 3SLS. The purpose of this approach is not to improve in‑sample fit, but to regularise weakly identified channels and to ensure that the estimated system remains economically interpretable and suitable for policy analysis. Importantly, calibration is applied incrementally and only where clearly warranted by the unrestricted results.

13.0.1 6.1 Hybrid A: Regularising weak demand slopes in price‑ and wage‑setting

The first calibration stage (Hybrid A) targets demand‑side slopes in the Phillips Curve and the Wage Phillips Curve. In the unrestricted system, these parameters are weakly identified and unstable in sign, reflecting the fact that observed inflation and wage dynamics over the sample are dominated by supply shocks, institutional features of the labour market, and monetary policy feedback, rather than by contemporaneous demand pressure.

Accordingly, Hybrid A fixes the output‑gap coefficient in the price Phillips Curve and the unemployment‑gap coefficient in the wage Phillips Curve at small, theoretically plausible values. These calibrations impose a weak demand pull in price- and wage‑setting without forcing textbook magnitudes or altering the overall structure of the system. All remaining parameters—including inflation persistence, wage inertia, policy reaction coefficients, and labor‑market dynamics—remain freely estimated.

This step regularises channels that the data are unable to identify cleanly on their own, while preserving the empirical character of the inflation and wage processes. Importantly, Hybrid A does not materially change the qualitative interpretation of the system; rather, it prevents weakly identified demand slopes from absorbing unrelated system‑level variation.

13.0.2 6.2 Hybrid B: Anchoring the level of monetary policy via the neutral real rate

The second calibration stage (Hybrid B) addresses a distinct identification issue: the level of the real policy stance. In quarterly macroeconomic data, the neutral real interest rate is notoriously difficult to estimate jointly with inflation dynamics, policy smoothing, and structural break effects. In the unrestricted system, this lack of level identification manifests itself as ambiguity in the interpretation of real‑rate movements and in the estimated demand–policy interaction.

Hybrid B therefore anchors the neutral real interest rate at an externally plausible value, effectively fixing the steady‑state level of the real rate while leaving all behavioral responses unconstrained. This normalization affects levels but not marginal dynamics, and it allows the real‑rate gap to be interpreted consistently across equations and simulations. Crucially, Hybrid B does not impose any restriction on the sensitivity of demand to interest rates; it simply removes a flat direction in the likelihood associated with the policy level.

At this stage, the model remains fully data‑driven in its dynamic responses, but becomes more transparent in its interpretation of monetary conditions.

13.0.3 6.3 Hybrid C: Enforcing monotone policy transmission for simulation purposes

While Hybrid B yields a coherent and empirically grounded system, impulse‑response analysis reveals that monetary policy shocks induce non‑monotone output adjustment: following an initial contraction, output temporarily rebounds before converging back to baseline. This behaviour reflects the dominance of endogenous policy reactions in the estimated IS block, which causes policy shocks to be interpreted partly as states correlated with strong demand rather than as causal disturbances.

For policy simulation purposes, this lack of monotonicity is problematic. A contractionary monetary shock should generate a uniformly contractionary path for real activity along the transition, even if the data do not cleanly separate reaction from transmission. Accordingly, the third calibration stage (Hybrid C) imposes a weakly negative interest‑rate elasticity in the IS curve. This constraint is deliberately modest and is introduced solely to ensure monotone and interpretable propagation of policy shocks.

Hybrid C does not alter the estimated policy rule, inflation dynamics, wage setting, or labor‑market structure. Its role is limited to enforcing causal discipline in the demand–policy channel so that the model can be used reliably for counterfactual experiments and scenario analysis. The resulting impulse responses exhibit smooth, monotone contraction in output following monetary tightening, while preserving the empirical narrative established in the earlier stages.

13.0.4 6.4 Summary

Taken together, the three calibration stages play distinct and complementary roles. Hybrid A regularises weakly identified demand slopes in nominal adjustment, Hybrid B anchors the level of monetary policy, and Hybrid C enforces monotone policy transmission for simulation purposes. Each stage is motivated directly by diagnostics from the preceding specification, and no calibration is imposed without clear empirical justification. The final hybrid system is therefore both empirically disciplined and policy‑usable, combining the strengths of system estimation with minimal, transparent theoretical structure.

16 Introduction

Our starting point is Scarth’s specification of the Mundell-Fleming model. We move on to consider rationally formed exchange rate expectations. Dornbusch (1976) shows that overshooting in the exchange rate can easily occur in a model in which exchange rates adjust at a faster pace than goods prices. Product prices that are sluggish (and cannot adjust in the short run as much as they can in the long run) can force more adjustment in the exchange rate in the short run than is required in the long run.

Dornbusch extends the perfect capital mobility version of the Mundell-Fleming model to allow for forward-looking exchange rate expectations and interprets the resulting overshooting as explaining exchange rate volatility. This is the subject of Scarth (1988), Ch. 9.2. For pedagogic reasons, Scarth deals with the supply-side effects of exchange rates and resolution of the stocks vs. flows in the balance of payments issue through separate models in which the restriction that was relaxed in the preceding analysis is re-applied in the subsequent analysis. This is where we depart from Scarth for the moment but he points us in the right direction, noting that Buiter and Miller (1982) have made significant progress in combining all of these features in one model.

Buiter and Miller’s NBER paper is impressive and was developed with the UK economy in mind. Specifically, Niehans (1983) in his comment on the paper notes that it can generally be regarded as an effort to apply the “underemployment version of Dornbusch’s exchange dynamics model to the stylised facts of British monetary policy”.

Estimated forward-looking IS relations tend to underperform simple autoregressive models and, quite often, little empirical support is found for the relationship between output and expected real interest rates.9 This is partly because the output gap moves with a degree of inertia that is inconsistent with the adjustment paths implied by forward-looking, rational expectations models. In these models, it is the rational but immediate adjustment of households’ expectations to innovations which implies a jump response in consumption, which, in practise, is rarely seen in the data.

Some attempts have been made to explain why consumption reacts so slowly in response to changes in interest rates. One such endeavour is the habit formation model of Fuhrer (2000). Fuhrer postulates that the utility derived from consumption depends both on the absolute level of consumption and the level of current consumption relative to past consumption – that households do not like consuming less than they have been and initially resist changes, before eventually adjusting. This modification was shown to substantially improve the fit of the model.10

Other work, predominantly concerned with why the behaviour of consumption appears to invalidate the permanent income hypothesis, such as Muellbauer (1988), suggests that households may be myopic in their consumption choices. Campbell and Mankiw (1989) offer the hypothesis that households do not have the resources to engage in producing full forecasts and so it is optimal for them to use a rule of thumb when updating their consumption plans in response to income shocks.

1 A structural macro-econometric model of the UK economy

The model is a hybrid New Keynesian system, defined by the following system of seven stochastic equations, following the semi-structural traditions of OBR(2012) where variables are defined in gap form (tilde) as deviations from their respective trends (using HP filters). A seven‑equation semi‑structural system with hybrid calibration–estimation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{IS (agg dem):} \quad \tilde{Y}_t &= \alpha_1 \tilde{Y}_{t-1} - \phi_r \tilde{r}_t - \phi_{i_{spr}} \tilde{i}^{spr}_t + \alpha_2 \Delta\text{NX}_t + \alpha_3 \Delta\text{RER}_{t-1} + \alpha_4 \text{BP}^Y_t + \alpha_5\text{BP}^r_t +\epsilon^{\text{IS}}_t \\ \text{PC (inflation):} \quad \tilde{\pi}_t &= \beta_1 \tilde{\pi}_{t-1} + \beta_2 \tilde{\pi}_{t-2} + \kappa_Y \tilde{Y}_t + \beta_3 \tilde{\pi}^{\text{IM}}_t + \beta_4 \tilde{\pi}^{\text{E}}_t + \beta_5 \text{BP}^{\pi}_t + \epsilon^{\text{PC}}_t \\ \text{TR (mon pol)}: \quad i_t &= \rho i_{t-1} + (1 - \rho) (r^* + \pi^* + \tau_{\pi} \tilde{\pi}_t + \tau_Y \tilde{Y}_t) + \epsilon^{\text{TR}}_t \\ \text{OLU (lab dem):} \quad \tilde{U}_t &= \delta_1 \tilde{U}_{t-1} - \delta_2 \tilde{Y}_t + \delta_3 \text{BP}^{U1}_t + \delta_4 \text{BP}^{U2}_t + \delta_5 \text{BP}^{U3}_t + \delta_6 \text{BP}^{U4}_t + \epsilon^{\text{OLU}}_t \\ \text{WPC (lab sup):} \quad \tilde{w}_t &= \theta_1 \tilde{w}_{t-1} + \lambda_{\pi} \tilde\pi_{t-1} + \lambda_U \tilde U_t + \theta_2 \tilde{P}_t + \epsilon^{\text{WPC}}_t \\ \text{OLP (produc):} \quad \tilde{P}_t &= \eta_1 \tilde{P}_{t-1} + \eta_2 \tilde{Y}_t + \epsilon^{\text{OLP}}_t \\ \text{UIP (exch rate):} \quad \Delta \text{RER}_t &= \mu_1 \Delta \text{RER}_{t-1} + \psi \tilde i^{diff}_t + \mu_2 \Delta \text{NX}_t + \mu_3 \tilde \pi^{\text{IM}}_t + \epsilon^{\text{UIP}}_t \end{aligned} \]

Calibrated parameters are given symbolic subscripts to represent the exogenous variable they are measuring the role and significance of. Estimated parameters are assigned numeric subscripts, representing their ordering in the econometric analysis of the structural equations.

1.1 The IS relation (aggregate demand)

This equation describes how cyclical output \((\tilde{Y})\) responds to monetary conditions, external demand, exchange‑rate movements, and structural breaks. The lagged output gap term \((\tilde{Y}_{t-1})\) captures the intrinsic persistence of domestic demand, reflecting adjustment costs and slow-moving expectations.

The real interest rate gap \((\tilde{r}_t)\) and the credit spread gap \((\tilde{i}^{spr}_t)\) influence output through the standard inter-temporal substitution and financial‑conditions channels: a higher real rate or a wider spread tightens financial conditions and depresses activity.

External demand is captured via changes in net exports \((d\text{NX}_t)\), which affect aggregate demand in a small open economy. The lagged real exchange rate \((\Delta\text{RER}_{t-1})\) term captures expenditure‑switching effects, reflecting how movements in relative prices affect competitiveness with a delay.

The break dummies \((\text{BP}^Y, \text{BP}^r)\) allow for shifts in the aggregate‑demand process associated with major structural changes in the UK economy, revealed and derived through our diagnostic testing of the data.

Together, these elements form a semi‑structural representation of the UK business cycle, linking demand to financial conditions and external shocks.

Estimated dynamics: \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3\)

Calibrated demand sensitivities: \(\phi_r, \phi_{i^{spr}}\)

Break dummies capture shifts in behaviour: \(\text{BP}^Y, \text{BP}^r\) represent, respectively, the output gap break (set at 2020 Q1) and the real interest rate break (set at 2022 Q1).

1.2 Phillips curve relation (domestic price inflation)

In this equation, \(\tilde{\pi}_t\) is the inflation deviation from the Bank of England’s target. Because we use a domestically-driven measure of inflation (annualised change in the quarterly GDP deflator), we set the target at 2.25% as the base case (equivalent to the 2% target for CPI inflation).

\(\tilde{\pi}^\text{IM}_t\) is an import price inflation gap, \(\tilde{\pi}^\text{E}_t\) an energy price inflation gap and \(\text{BP}^{\pi}_t\) is a dummy variable representing the structural break in the inflation regime in 2022 Q1.

The inflation equation links price dynamics to their own persistence, domestic slack, and external cost‑ push forces. The autoregressive terms reflect backward‑looking elements of price setting and the empirical inertia of UK inflation. The output gap enters through the slope parameter, representing the degree to which domestic economic slack influences inflationary pressure. Import price inflation and energy‑price shocks capture external cost‑push channels — key drivers of inflation in an open economy like the UK. The structural‑break dummies account for shifts in inflation dynamics due to major regime changes, such as the global financial crisis and the post‑COVID inflation shock. This specification results in a hybrid Phillips Curve where domestic cyclical pressure plays a modest role, while external price shocks and persistence dominate short‑run inflation movements.

Inflation persistence parameters \((\beta_1, \beta_2)\) are estimated. Import and energy price pass‑through \((\beta_3, \beta_4)\) are also estimated. The Phillips curve slope \((\kappa_Y)\) is calibrated (small but positive, reflecting the evidence and conclusions of policymakers of a flattening of the Phillips curve in the UK).

1.3 Taylor rule (monetary policy / CB reaction function)

The Taylor Rule describes how the central bank sets the policy interest rate in response to economic conditions. Policy inertia is captured by the coefficient \((\rho)\) on the lagged nominal policy rate, reflecting gradualism in the Monetary Policy Committee’s decision-making and the desire to avoid unnecessary volatility in financial markets.

The central bank reacts to deviations of inflation from its target and to the output gap, with calibrated coefficients \((\tau_{\pi},\tau_Y)\) ensuring determinacy and policy realism.

The neutral real interest rate \((r^*)\) and the inflation target \((\pi^*)\) anchor the long‑run level of the policy rate.

This equation captures the systematic component of monetary policy in the UK’s inflation‑targeting regime, in which \(\rho\) (policy inertia) is estimated.

For OLS purposes (diagnostic stage), we estimate the reduced‑form hybrid rule $ i_t = 0 + i{t-1} + _{} t + {Y} _t + _t $ in which \(\gamma_{\pi} = (1-\rho)\tau_{\pi}\) and \(\gamma_Y = (1-\rho)\tau_Y\)

1.4 Okun’s Law for unemployment (labour market slack)

The Okun equation links labour‑market slack \((\tilde{U}_t)\) to the business cycle. The lagged unemployment gap \((\tilde{U}_{t-1})\) reflects persistence in labour‑market conditions, which adjust more slowly than output.

The negative output‑gap coefficient \((-\delta_2)\) embodies the traditional Okun’s Law relationship: when the economy operates above potential, the unemployment gap closes; when output is weak, labour-market slack widens.

Structural‑break dummies \((\text{BP}^{U1} \space \text{to} \space \text{BP}^{U4})\) allow for long‑run shifts in the natural rate of unemployment or in the responsiveness of unemployment to output. This specification recognises that the UK labour market has undergone substantial structural changes over the past two decades and embeds these into the dynamics of unemployment.

\(\text{BP}^{U1}\) is the 2004 Q2 labour‑market break

\(\text{BP}^{U2}\) is the GFC 2008 Q4 break

\(\text{BP}^{U3}\) is the 2013 Q4 break

\(\text{BP}^{U4}\) is the COVID-19 break (2019 Q4 / 2020 Q1)

These were the four statistically confirmed unemployment‑gap breaks.

All parameters are estimated. No calibration planned [and hopefully not needed] here.

1.5 Wage Phillips curve (labour supply)

The Wage Phillips Curve governs the dynamics of real wage pressure. Wage growth exhibits persistence through the lagged wage gap term \((\tilde{w}_{t-1})\), reflecting staggered wage contracts and adaptive expectations among firms and workers.

Labour-market tightness enters through the unemployment gap \((\tilde{U}_t)\), following standard bargaining or matching‑function models in which tighter labour markets raise wage pressure.

Expected inflation (deviation from target) \([\text{E}_t(\tilde{\pi}_{t+1}) = \tilde{\pi}_{t-1}]\) enters as an exogenous driver of nominal wage demands, ensuring that wage formation remains consistent with long‑run inflation targets.

The productivity gap \((\tilde{P}_t)\) term captures how deviations in trend productivity influence wage-setting behaviour, consistent with labour‑cost markups.

[[[[[[ [This equation therefore links wage inflation to both cyclical labour-market conditions and long‑run institutional shifts. Break dummies account for major regime changes — including shifts in bargaining power, labour mobility, and post‑pandemic tightness — which have altered the wage-setting process. ]]]]]]

Where: the wage Phillips curve slopes \((\lambda_{\pi}, \lambda_U)\) are calibrated whilst the dynamic persistence and productivity coefficients \((\theta_1, \theta_2)\) are estimated from the data.

The wage‑gap series is clean enough to estimate dynamically without heavy structural adjustments. The equation should be stable and well‑behaved with one or no break dummies, a standard lag structure and calibrated slope parameters.

1.6 Okun’s Law for productivity

The Okun equation models cyclical productivity as a function of its own persistence and contemporaneous output. Productivity tends to be highly persistent due to slow-moving technological and organisational adjustments. The positive output‑gap coefficient reflects procyclical productivity: during expansions, firms utilise capital more intensively, and labour inputs become more efficient; whereas during downturns, capacity utilisation falls. The structural‑break terms capture the well‑documented shifts in the UK’s productivity regime — including the post‑2008 productivity slowdown and post‑COVID adjustments. This equation provides a parsimonious representation of productivity fluctuations without specifying technology shocks explicitly.

This is stable, simple, and consistent with macro theory.

1.7 Uncovered interest parity (UIP)

The real‑exchange‑rate equation links currency movements to interest‑rate differentials and external flows. The autoregressive term captures the persistent nature of real exchange rate dynamics.

The nominal interest‑rate differential gap \(\tilde{i}^{diff}_t\) reflects the uncovered‑interest‑parity (UIP) condition, with \(\psi\) calibrated to reflect its empirical weakness in UK data.

\(\tilde{i}^{diff}_t = (i - i^f) - (i - i^f)^{trend}\), in which \(i\) is the official BOE rate and \(i^f\) is a trade-weighted average of the official central bank rates of the UK’s top 5 trading partners (the Euro Area, USA, China, Switzerland and Norway). This is consistent with our approach to the real exchange rate and our proxy for foreign price levels.

Deviations in net exports influence the real exchange rate through trade‑balance adjustments and the demand for domestic versus foreign currency. Import‑price inflation enters to reflect the import‑cost channel, whereby external cost shocks affect relative prices.

This equation provides a stable reduced‑form representation of exchange‑rate dynamics that accommodates both UIP and non‑UIP forces.

Dummies may be added but often UIP is left parsimonious.

2 Estimation Strategy

The estimation of the semi‑structural macroeconomic model proceeds in several structured stages.
The goal is to ensure that each behavioural equation is statistically well‑specified, dynamically coherent, and suitable for joint estimation within a system that accounts for cross‑equation relationships and endogeneity.

The overall approach follows standard practice in central‑bank semi‑structural modelling:
(i) single‑equation OLS diagnostics → (ii) limited‑information IV estimation (2SLS) → (iii) full‑information system estimation (3SLS).

2.1 Single‑Equation OLS Diagnostics

As a first step, each behavioural equation is estimated individually using ordinary least squares (OLS).
OLS estimates are not used for inference or for constructing the final parameter set.
Instead, the OLS stage serves four essential diagnostic purposes:

  1. Sign and magnitude validation Coefficients are checked against theoretical priors (e.g., negative response of unemployment to the output gap; positive inflation persistence).

  2. Dynamic specification validation OLS estimates confirm the appropriate lag structure, consistent with ACF/PACF analysis performed earlier (e.g., AR(1) dynamics in the output and unemployment gaps).

  3. Residual behaviour assessment Breusch–Godfrey tests (for serial correlation), Breusch–Pagan tests (for heteroskedasticity), visual residual diagnostics (plots, ACF, distributions). These tests ensure that residual patterns are consistent with theoretical expectations and that no additional dynamics or break adjustments are needed.

  4. Identification of equations affected by simultaneity The Phillips Curve and Wage Phillips Curve exhibit classic endogeneity patterns (wrong signs on output/unemployment gaps under OLS and significant autocorrelation in residuals). This confirms the necessity of instrumental‑variables estimation in the next stage.

The remaining equations (IS, Okun, Productivity, UIP, and the Taylor Rule) display well‑behaved OLS diagnostics, validating their structural form.

2.2 Limited‑Information Instrumental‑Variables Estimation (2SLS)

OLS estimation is inconsistent when regressors are endogenous. Based on the OLS diagnostic stage, two equations require instrumental‑variables treatment:

  • Price Phillips Curve — output gap is endogenous through joint determination with inflation.

  • Wage Phillips Curve — unemployment is endogenous due to labour‑market simultaneity, and the COVID furlough episode amplifies the bias.

For each of these equations, we estimate a Two‑Stage Least Squares (2SLS) model using only the equation of interest (“limited‑information” IV estimation). This involves:

  1. Identifying endogenous regressors
-   Phillips Curve: Y~t\tilde{Y}_tY~t​

-    Wage Phillips Curve: U~t\tilde{U}_tU~t​
  1. Constructing an instrument set from:
-   lagged values of gaps (e.g. Y_t−2Y\_{t-2}Y_t−2, U_t−2U\_{t-2}U_t−2),

-    predetermined financial variables (e.g. real‑rate gap, yield‑spread gap),

-    external inflation measures (import and energy inflation gaps),

-    break dummies,

-    lagged exchange‑rate and trade variables.
  1. Estimating each equation separately using ivreg() This provides
-    consistent coefficient estimates,

-    a correction for simultaneity bias,

-    and cleaned‑up residual dynamics suitable for system estimation.

This stage ensures that, prior to system estimation, the most problematic equations (the Phillips Curves) are already structurally identified.

2.3 Full‑Information System Estimation (3SLS)

With the structural equations validated and simultaneity addressed, we estimate the entire 7‑equation model jointly using Three‑Stage Least Squares (3SLS) via the systemfit() framework.

Full‑information estimation is appropriate for this model because:

  1. Equations are linked through shared endogenous variables (e.g. output gap interacts with inflation, real interest rates, unemployment, wages).

  2. Cross‑equation error correlations carry useful information e.g., cost‑push inflation shocks (import prices, energy) often correlate with exchange‑rate and real‑rate shocks.

  3. System estimation improves efficiency 3SLS uses the entire estimated covariance matrix of residuals to weight each equation appropriately.

  4. Structural identification is system‑wide Parameter calibration (e.g., Taylor Rule coefficients, Okun elasticity) can be imposed consistently across the entire system.

  5. The resulting shocks are system-consistent Each equation produces structural errors that should be interpretable as demand shocks, supply shocks, monetary shocks, wage shocks, etc.

3SLS therefore provides joint estimation, improved precision, correction for remaining endogeneity, and a coherent set of system‑consistent shocks for analysis and forecasting.

2.4 Calibration and Normalization

A small number of coefficients are calibrated rather than estimated freely in the system, including:

  • selected policy‑rule coefficients in the Taylor Rule,

  • small elasticity parameters where identification is weak (e.g., IS curve’s long-term spread term),

  • steady‑state relationships (e.g., neutral real interest rate, inflation target).

Calibrated values are standard in semi‑structural models; they improve identification and stabilise system estimation.

The hybrid model works because some things are stable and estimable, while others are unstable and should be calibrated.

2.5 Final Estimation Output

The final estimation output from the 3SLS stage yields:

  • system‑consistent structural parameters,

  • cleaned‑up structural shocks,

  • a coherent mapping between the theoretical model and the empirical data,

  • well‑behaved residuals with minimal autocorrelation,

  • economically interpretable dynamics consistent with macro theory.

This parameter set forms the basis for:

  • impulse‑response analysis,

  • historical shock decomposition,

  • counterfactual simulations,

  • and model‑based forecasting.

3 Model calibration

3.1 Parameters for ESTIMATION vs. CALIBRATION

Estimate things that are strongly tied to the cycle, stable across long periods, driven by persistence, like: Lag coefficients (in IS, Phillips, wage, RER, Okun, Taylor), Okun’s law elasticity (ΔU_GAP vs Y_GAP), Inflation and wage growth persistence; Import / energy price pass‑through / external price shocks; ΔRER persistence; Dummy effects (COVID, GFC).

Calibrate things that are: theoretically meaningful, unstable over time, too noisy to estimate, small sample / break‑sensitive, like Phillips Curve slope w.r.t output gap, Wage Phillips curve slope w.r.t tightness, Taylor Rule inflation response coefficient, Taylor Rule output gap response, Productivity trend, UIP coefficient (rate differential), long‑run RER elasticity. These are fragile in estimation but straightforward to calibrate. Central banks calibrate these because they know they can’t be estimated reliably.

Need initial values for:

  • \(\phi_r, \phi_{i^{spr}}\) IS curve slope(s)

  • \(\kappa_Y\) Phillips Curve slope;

  • \(\lambda_U\) Wage PC slope;

  • \(\tau_{\pi}, \tau_Y\) Taylor Rule slopes.

  • \(\psi\) UIP slope

These can be chosen from: BoE papers, IMF models, OECD semi‑structural models, literature values (typical ranges).

3.2 Phillips Curve slope \((\kappa_Y)\)

Recommended UK calibration: 0.02–0.10. The Bank of England’s recent research suggests the slope is extremely flat, reflecting both macro data and micro evidence: BoE Staff Working Paper “How curvy is the Phillips curve?” (2025) finds inflation responds only weakly to slack, even at the micro level, indicating a very flat Phillips Curve for the UK. [bankofengland.co.uk]. The Federal Reserve’s 2024 review on slope estimation highlights κ values typically below 0.1 in advanced economies, consistent with the UK experience post‑2008. [federalreserve.gov] The ECB’s 2025 Occasional Paper on Phillips curves reports similarly small slopes for Europe, consistent with fewer price adjustments and supply shocks dominating inflation dynamics. [ecb.europa.eu] Practical recommendation for hybrid model: κᵧ between 0.03 and 0.07. Makes Phillips Curve “work” without forcing unrealistic sensitivity[?].

3.3 Wage Phillips Curve slope \((\lambda_U)\)

Recommended UK calibration: 0.15–0.40. The BoE 2025 “How curvy is the Phillips curve?” paper finds stronger convexity in wage price‑setting than in goods inflation, especially after Covid. [bankofengland.co.uk] Bank of England forecasting speeches (e.g., by Pill, 2023) emphasise that labour‑market tightness has become the dominant domestic inflation driver post‑2021, implying a larger slope on unemployment gap in wage equations. [ecb.europa.eu] UK micro data show wage responses are stronger than price responses, consistent with studies at BoE and ONS (via their labour cost models). Practical recommendation: λᵤ ≈ 0.25 is a very standard semi‑structural calibration for the UK.

3.4 Taylor Rule inflation response \((\tau_{\pi})\)

Recommended calibration: 1.3–2.0: UK semi‑structural rules used internally at the BoE have historically assumed \(\tau_{\pi}\) around 1.5, consistent with Taylor (1993) and UK monetary policy practice. ECB’s 2025 modelling guide highlights values >1 as necessary for determinacy and shows most semi‑structural models cluster around 1.5–2.0. [ecb.europa.eu] Macro modelling reviews (Economics Observatory 2024) report UK models in OBR, OECD and IMF often use- 1.3–1.7 for the inflation coefficient as part of calibration practice. [economicso…vatory.com] Practical recommendation: Use \(\tau_{\pi} = 1.5\)unless there is strong evidence of a weaker or stronger response.

3.5 Taylor Rule output gap response \((\tau_Y)\)

Recommended calibration: 0.1–0.5: UK practice typically involves a small output-gap coefficient. ECB semi‑structural model comparisons show values 0.1–0.3 are the most used. [ecb.europa.eu] UK academic small‑macro models (Garratt et al., 1998/1999; cited in Cambridge VAR framework) use response coefficients in the 0.1–0.4 range for output in monetary rules. [files.econ.cam.ac.uk]. Practical recommendation: \(\tau_Y = 0.2\)(the most common UK value).

3.6 Real neutral interest rate \((r^*)\)

Recommended calibration: 0.0% to 1.0% real. Post‑GFC UK \(r^*\) estimates from the Bank of England trend toward 0 to 0.5% real, with some upward drift after 2022. ECB and OECD semi‑structural forecasting references typically put medium‑run \(r^*\) for advanced economies between 0 and 1% (ECB Occasional Papers). [ecb.europa.eu] Practical recommendation: Use \(r^*=0.25 - 0.50%\) in your calibration. (If inflation expectations are anchored at 2%, the implied nominal neutral rate is ~2.25–2.50%.)

3.7 IS Curve interest‑rate gap coefficient \((\phi_r)\)

Recommended calibration: 0.05–0.20. Semi‑structural VAR models for the UK (BoE 2025 Macro Technical Paper No.3) show relatively modest effects of interest rates on output in the short run (low elasticity). [bankofengland.co.uk] Comparison across ESCB models shows IS curves often calibrate \((\phi_r)\) between 0.1 and 0.2 (ECB Occasional Paper No.377). [ecb.europa.eu]. Practical recommendation: Set \((\phi_r)\) = 0.15.

3.8 UIP coefficient on interest‑rate differential \((\psi)\)

Recommended calibration: 0.05–0.20. UIP “fails” empirically for the UK (low pass‑through), so full estimation is unstable. BoE models typically impose small calibrated values to preserve structure, consistent with international practice. No direct citation in your results lists UIP calibration for UK specifically, but consistent evidence appears in the ECB and macro modelling literature that semi‑structural models heavily down‑weight UIP (covered in Occasional Paper No.377). [ecb.europa.eu] Practical recommendation: \((\psi)=0.10\) (safe, modest UIP effect).

5 Annex II - Data diagnostics

This annex covers the cleaning and diagnostic testing of the data for each variable, including stationarity and structural‑break testing, de-trending, transformation into gaps or first differences, multicollinearity diagnostics, autocorrelation and lag structure assessment, as well as scaling and economic plausibility checks. In applying these diagnostic procedures to the quarterly macroeconomic time series data prior to model estimation, the objective was to ensure that all endogenous and exogenous variables are appropriately transformed, stationary, free from undue multicollinearity and that they exhibit time‑series properties consistent with their theoretical roles within the system.

5.1 Stationarity Testing

All macroeconomic variables were transformed into either gap form (deviations from trend or from steady‑state values) or first differences where required by theory. Stationarity was evaluated using the Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test for each transformed series.

All variables exhibit stationarity at conventional significance levels. The output gap \((\tilde{Y})\), unemployment gap \((\tilde{U})\), inflation deviation \((\tilde{\pi})\), wage gap \((\tilde{w})\), productivity gap \((\tilde{P})\), real interest‑rate gap \((\tilde{r})\), 5‑year spread gap \((\tilde{i}^{spr})\), import‑price inflation gap \((\tilde{\pi}^{\text{IM}})\), and energy‑price inflation gap \((\tilde{\pi}^{\text{E}})\) were all stationary (with ADF \(p<0.05\) in each case).

Differenced variables include the change in the real exchange rate \((\Delta\text{RER})\) and the change in net exports \((\Delta\text{NX})\). These are stationary by construction. The raw nominal interest‑rate differential \((i-i^f)\) was found to be non‑stationary, so a trend‑adjusted differential gap was constructed, yielding a stationary series suitable for use in the UIP equation.

5.2 Structural‑Break Testing

To ensure robustness of stationarity and to identify potential regime shifts, each series was tested for breaks using Zivot–Andrews one‑break unit‑root tests and Bai–Perron multiple‑break tests.

We found the output gap to have a single structural break in 2020Q1 (COVID‑19 collapse). Inflation deviation and the real rate gap appear to show a coinciding structural break around 2021–22, corresponding to the inflation regime shift. Analysis of the unemployment gap revealed four persistent structural breaks (2004Q2, 2008Q4, 2013Q4, 2019Q4). For all other variables, Bai–Perron BICs were minimized at zero breaks.

Corresponding break dummies were included only in the equations whose dependent variables exhibited genuine structural breaks, thus seeking parsimony and stability.

5.3 Scaling and Economic Plausibility

All variables were inspected to ensure the magnitudes were economically plausible and appropriately scaled. Gaps (output, unemployment, real wages, productivity) lie in the expected ±1–5% range. ΔRER and ΔNX display reasonable quarterly volatility. Import and energy inflation gaps show large swings during 2019–2023, reflecting documented global cost‑push shocks. The real interest‑rate gap behaved consistently with monetary‑policy and inflation dynamics. No series exhibited signs of mis‑scaling, measurement errors, or inappropriate de-trending.

5.4 Multicollinearity Diagnostics

For each equation, pairwise correlations and Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) were examined for all regressors, including lagged dependent variables and calibrated regressors. Pairwise correlations across all equations were modest (typically < 0.4). Inflation lags showed expected moderate correlation (~0.7), but well below problematic thresholds. Import‑ and energy‑price inflation gaps were moderately correlated (~0.6), consistent with common external shocks. All VIF values were well below conventional thresholds (VIF < 5), indicating no multicollinearity concerns. The regressor sets are therefore suitable for stable 3SLS estimation.

5.5 Persistence and Lag Structure (ACF/PACF Analysis)

Autocorrelation (ACF) and partial autocorrelation (PACF) plots were examined to confirm appropriate dynamic structure:

Gaps (output, unemployment, wages, productivity, real interest‑rate) demonstrated clear AR(1) processes, confirming use of a single lag in the IS, Okun, Wage‑Phillips, and productivity equations.

Inflation Deviation shows a AR(1)–AR(2) structure, validating inclusion of two inflation lags in the Phillips Curve.

ΔRER and ΔNX both showed minimal autocorrelation beyond lag 1 with no strong case for additional dynamics to be added.

Import and Energy Price Gaps: AR(1)–AR(2) persistence but no evidence of higher‑order dynamics.

No variable exhibited long‑memory behaviour or seasonality requiring deseasonalisation.

5.6 Outliers and Shock Episodes

Visual inspection and residual diagnostics identified several periods of extreme volatility, including COVID‑19 (2020Q1-Q2), the post‑pandemic inflation shock (2021–22) and commodity‑price spikes (2022–23). These episodes correspond precisely to detected structural breaks and are handled through appropriately placed break dummies in the relevant equations.

5.7 Final Assessment

Diagnostic checks would appear to confirm that all measures have been taken to appropriately filter, difference, or lag-augment all data series and to ensure that the data are stationary, appropriately de-trended, and well-scaled. Structural breaks have been carefully identified and incorporated only where justified. No problematic multicollinearity exists within the regressor matrices and the dynamics implied by ACF/PACF plots are reflected in the model specification. With these measures, we are confident the dataset is therefore in excellent condition for equation‑by‑equation estimation and subsequent 3SLS system estimation, ensuring valid inference and robust structural relationships.

7 Annex IV - 2SLS analysis of PC and WPC relations

This stage targets specific equations where regressors are endogenous, namely the Phillips Curve and wage Phillips Curve

Both failed the BG test under OLS, indicating serial correlation due to endogenous regressors. The Y_GAP coefficient in the PC had the wrong sign under OLS, classic sign of simultaneity. The u_GAP coefficient in the WPC had the wrong sign, with unemployment and wage setting jointly determined, a classic OLS failure. OLS residuals remain autocorrelated even after the correct lag structure → the model is correct, but OLS is the wrong estimator. Other equations do not suffer from strong simultaneity. IS, Okun, Productivity, UIP, TR can be left to later in the system stage.

2SLS is used to repair the demand/supply blocks before we combine all equations in a 3SLS estimator. This involves instrumenting the endogenous variables using relevant instruments drawn from the full data set (e.g., lags of exogenous variables, predetermined variables, etc.):

  • in the Phillips curve, the endogenous regressors include the output gap \(\tilde{Y}_t\) and possibly the import price inflation gap \(\tilde{\pi}^{\text{IM}}_t\), even if less prominent. The (adaptive) expectations proxy \([\text{E}_t(\tilde{\pi}_{t+1}) = \tilde{\pi}_{t-1}]\) is pre-determined;

  • in the Wage Phillips Curve, the endogenous regressors are the unemployment gap \((\tilde{U}_t)\) and possibly \(\tilde{\pi}_{t-1}\). The lagged wage gap \((\tilde{w}_{t-1})\) is predetermined.

7.1 Instruments for 2SLS estimation

We use predetermined / exogenous variables such as: lags of output gap, lags of unemployment gap, lags of wage gap, r_GAP, i_DIFFL_GAP, drer, dNX, IMcpi_GAP, ecpi_GAP, the structural break dummies.

Instrument relevance and validity were assessed using first‑stage F‑statistics, partial R2R^2R2, Wu–Hausman endogeneity tests, and over‑identification tests, with lag depth chosen to balance relevance and exogeneity based on dynamic diagnostics.

REMINDER: 2SLS replaces an endogenous variable by the part of it that can be explained by exogenous drivers, thereby isolating the causal channel of interest. OR 2SLS is like solving the system for the exogenous component of an endogenous variable and substituting that component into the structural equation.

This is not the final Phillips Curve as this is only limited-information IV. In 2SLS, we are conditioning on current wages, policy reaction, external prices but not allowing feedback via IS, Okun, wage setting or UIP. The full structural feedback should appears in 3SLS, as part of which policy reaction and demand are jointly determined, output, wages, and prices interact simultaneously, the PC slope often shrinks toward zero or becomes mildly positive. 8 Annex V - 3SLS system estimation

Up to now, we have engaged with OLS for diagnostics and structure checking and 2SLS for fixing local endogeneity equation‑by‑equation. Now, with 3SLS:, we estimate all equations jointly, allow cross‑equation error correlation, impose system‑wide identification, stop asking “is this regressor exogenous here?” and start asking “is this variable exogenous in the system?”

This is crucial because the model is explicitly simultaneous: output ↔︎ inflation ↔︎ policy ↔︎ real rates; output ↔︎ unemployment ↔︎ wages ↔︎ inflation; inflation ↔︎ exchange rate ↔︎ import prices. 3SLS is the first estimator that actually respects this structure. Mechanically, 3SLS combines 2SLS logic (endogenous RHS variables are instrumented) and SUR logic (residuals allowed to be correlated across equations). So it answers: “Using only exogenous variation, and accounting for cross‑equation covariance, what is the system‑consistent set of parameters?”

That’s why it is the final estimator.

Once both PC and WPC are fixed via 2SLS, you estimate the whole 7‑equation system jointly, allowing:

  • cross‑equation error covariances

  • structural identification

  • system‑consistent parameters

  • higher efficiency

  • and correction of any remaining endogeneity

You can assemble them into a system of equations and estimate them using 3SLS:

  • ✔ correcting remaining endogeneity

  • ✔ exploiting cross-equation error correlations

  • ✔ obtaining system‑consistent shocks

  • ✔ improving efficiency

  • ✔ producing your final structural parameter set

“Fully free 3SLS” (the first 3SLS run) answers one question only: “If I let the data speak as freely as possible, once simultaneity and cross‑equation correlations are handled, what do the structural relationships want to look like?” This step is analog‑diagnostic, not policy‑ready. It is used to check signs across the whole system, gauge magnitudes relative to priors, see which slopes are weakly identified, see which parameters are trying to do too much work, identify trade‑offs (e.g. PC slope vs policy response); confirm that your structure is coherent once all feedback loops are closed.

At this stage: you do not over‑interpret significance; you expect some coefficients to be noisy; you expect some slopes to be implausible in magnitude. That’s normal — and informative.

Why you don’t start with calibration: If you calibrated before seeing free 3SLS results, you would not know whether: the calibration is supporting the data, or the calibration is suppressing a deeper misspecification. Free 3SLS acts like an X‑ray: it shows where the bones are weak before you put on the cast.

What the hybrid (calibrated) model is doing: once you’ve seen the unrestricted 3SLS results, you then ask: “Which parameters do I actually want the data to identify, and which should be disciplined by theory or external evidence?” Typical examples (very familiar from central‑bank models):

  • Phillips‑curve output slope (κᵧ): often weakly identified → calibrated or tightly constrained

  • Wage Phillips Curve unemployment slope (λᵤ): small and noisy in data → calibrated

  • IS curve semi‑elasticities of long rates: calibrated for stability

  • Policy reaction coefficients: either calibrated or gently constrained

  • Trend / steady‑state parameters: almost always calibrated

The hybrid model therefore: reduces variance where the data are weak, improves numerical stability, prevents trade‑offs between poorly identified slopes, makes counterfactuals interpretable, anchors the model to theory and institutional knowledge. Crucially: You are not calibrating to “improve fit” — you are calibrating to resolve identification problems revealed by free estimation.

We began by estimating the unrestricted system via 3SLS to assess identification and sign consistency. Given weak identification of some structural slopes, we then impose calibrated values for selected parameters in line with standard macroeconomic priors, re‑estimating the remaining parameters using a constrained system estimator.

What we have now is not a collection of regressions, but a system-consistent empirical macro model.

10 Annex VII - Hybrid estimation and calibration strategy

The unrestricted 3SLS results serve their intended diagnostic purpose. They identify which parameters are robustly pinned down by the data once simultaneity and cross‑equation feedbacks are fully accounted for, and which parameters are weakly identified or absorb system‑level interactions. In particular, demand‑side slopes in the price‑ and wage‑setting blocks are imprecisely estimated once monetary policy reactions are endogenised, reflecting both the dominance of large supply shocks in the sample and the strong systematic response of policy to macroeconomic conditions.

These findings motivate a hybrid estimation strategy, in which a small subset of behavioural parameters is disciplined using external evidence and theoretical priors, while the remaining parameters are estimated jointly using constrained 3SLS. The purpose of this approach is not to improve in‑sample fit, but to regularise weakly identified channels and to ensure that the estimated system remains economically interpretable and suitable for policy analysis. Importantly, calibration is applied incrementally and only where clearly warranted by the unrestricted results.

10.0.1 6.1 Hybrid A: Regularising weak demand slopes in price‑ and wage‑setting

The first calibration stage (Hybrid A) targets demand‑side slopes in the Phillips Curve and the Wage Phillips Curve. In the unrestricted system, these parameters are weakly identified and unstable in sign, reflecting the fact that observed inflation and wage dynamics over the sample are dominated by supply shocks, institutional features of the labour market, and monetary policy feedback, rather than by contemporaneous demand pressure.

Accordingly, Hybrid A fixes the output‑gap coefficient in the price Phillips Curve and the unemployment‑gap coefficient in the wage Phillips Curve at small, theoretically plausible values. These calibrations impose a weak demand pull in price- and wage‑setting without forcing textbook magnitudes or altering the overall structure of the system. All remaining parameters—including inflation persistence, wage inertia, policy reaction coefficients, and labor‑market dynamics—remain freely estimated.

This step regularises channels that the data are unable to identify cleanly on their own, while preserving the empirical character of the inflation and wage processes. Importantly, Hybrid A does not materially change the qualitative interpretation of the system; rather, it prevents weakly identified demand slopes from absorbing unrelated system‑level variation.

10.0.2 6.2 Hybrid B: Anchoring the level of monetary policy via the neutral real rate

The second calibration stage (Hybrid B) addresses a distinct identification issue: the level of the real policy stance. In quarterly macroeconomic data, the neutral real interest rate is notoriously difficult to estimate jointly with inflation dynamics, policy smoothing, and structural break effects. In the unrestricted system, this lack of level identification manifests itself as ambiguity in the interpretation of real‑rate movements and in the estimated demand–policy interaction.

Hybrid B therefore anchors the neutral real interest rate at an externally plausible value, effectively fixing the steady‑state level of the real rate while leaving all behavioral responses unconstrained. This normalization affects levels but not marginal dynamics, and it allows the real‑rate gap to be interpreted consistently across equations and simulations. Crucially, Hybrid B does not impose any restriction on the sensitivity of demand to interest rates; it simply removes a flat direction in the likelihood associated with the policy level.

At this stage, the model remains fully data‑driven in its dynamic responses, but becomes more transparent in its interpretation of monetary conditions.

10.0.3 6.3 Hybrid C: Enforcing monotone policy transmission for simulation purposes

While Hybrid B yields a coherent and empirically grounded system, impulse‑response analysis reveals that monetary policy shocks induce non‑monotone output adjustment: following an initial contraction, output temporarily rebounds before converging back to baseline. This behaviour reflects the dominance of endogenous policy reactions in the estimated IS block, which causes policy shocks to be interpreted partly as states correlated with strong demand rather than as causal disturbances.

For policy simulation purposes, this lack of monotonicity is problematic. A contractionary monetary shock should generate a uniformly contractionary path for real activity along the transition, even if the data do not cleanly separate reaction from transmission. Accordingly, the third calibration stage (Hybrid C) imposes a weakly negative interest‑rate elasticity in the IS curve. This constraint is deliberately modest and is introduced solely to ensure monotone and interpretable propagation of policy shocks.

Hybrid C does not alter the estimated policy rule, inflation dynamics, wage setting, or labor‑market structure. Its role is limited to enforcing causal discipline in the demand–policy channel so that the model can be used reliably for counterfactual experiments and scenario analysis. The resulting impulse responses exhibit smooth, monotone contraction in output following monetary tightening, while preserving the empirical narrative established in the earlier stages.

10.0.4 6.4 Summary

Taken together, the three calibration stages play distinct and complementary roles. Hybrid A regularises weakly identified demand slopes in nominal adjustment, Hybrid B anchors the level of monetary policy, and Hybrid C enforces monotone policy transmission for simulation purposes. Each stage is motivated directly by diagnostics from the preceding specification, and no calibration is imposed without clear empirical justification. The final hybrid system is therefore both empirically disciplined and policy‑usable, combining the strengths of system estimation with minimal, transparent theoretical structure.

11 Introduction

Our starting point is Scarth’s specification of the Mundell-Fleming model. We move on to consider rationally formed exchange rate expectations. Dornbusch (1976) shows that overshooting in the exchange rate can easily occur in a model in which exchange rates adjust at a faster pace than goods prices. Product prices that are sluggish (and cannot adjust in the short run as much as they can in the long run) can force more adjustment in the exchange rate in the short run than is required in the long run.

Dornbusch extends the perfect capital mobility version of the Mundell-Fleming model to allow for forward-looking exchange rate expectations and interprets the resulting overshooting as explaining exchange rate volatility. This is the subject of Scarth (1988), Ch. 9.2. For pedagogic reasons, Scarth deals with the supply-side effects of exchange rates and resolution of the stocks vs. flows in the balance of payments issue through separate models in which the restriction that was relaxed in the preceding analysis is re-applied in the subsequent analysis. This is where we depart from Scarth for the moment but he points us in the right direction, noting that Buiter and Miller (1982) have made significant progress in combining all of these features in one model.

Buiter and Miller’s NBER paper is impressive and was developed with the UK economy in mind. Specifically, Niehans (1983) in his comment on the paper notes that it can generally be regarded as an effort to apply the “underemployment version of Dornbusch’s exchange dynamics model to the stylised facts of British monetary policy”.

The paper, titled Real Exchange Rate Overshooting and the Output Cost of Bringing Down Inflation, develops a structural model to explain the UK’s experience with high interest rates and a strong pound. It provides a continuous-time framework for analysing exchange rate and inflation dynamics. The core of the model consists of five key equations covering money market equilibrium, aggregate demand, price evolution, core inflation, and uncovered interest parity. The model, a variation of the Dornbusch overshooting model, features sticky prices and jump variables, with nominal exchange rates and trend inflation adjusting instantly to shocks.

While it is of particular relevance to the Thatcher-era reforms, the work remains a foundational reference for modern structural analyses of the UK economy. The principles of the Buiter-Miller model (nominal stickiness with rational expectations in financial markets) using updated New Keynesian DSGE frameworks. The OBR’s “Small Model of the UK Economy” (used to simulate fiscal and monetary shocks) might reasonably be viewed as a contemporary structural successor, incorporating similar elements like exchange rate pass-through and inflation persistence, but evolving the Buiter-Miller logic into a four-equation New Keynesian framework. We have tethered our analysis to the OBR framework, but have sought to adapt it where required to achieve economic coherence and a better fit to the particular extract of UK data that we are using.

13.3 ‘Same old’ challenges with rational expectations

[CONFIRM WE ENCOUNTERED THIS PROBLEM IN DEVELOPING 3SLS…] The second criticism of the IS relation concerns the assumption of rational expectations. Estimated forward-looking IS relations tend to underperform simple autoregressive models and, quite often, little empirical support is found for the relationship between output and expected real interest rates.9 This is partly because the output gap moves with a degree of inertia that is inconsistent with the adjustment paths implied by forward-looking, rational expectations models. In these models, it is the rational but immediate adjustment of households’ expectations to innovations which implies a jump response in consumption, which, in practise, is rarely seen in the data.

Some attempts have been made to explain why consumption reacts so slowly in response to changes in interest rates. One such endeavour is the habit formation model of Fuhrer (2000). Fuhrer postulates that the utility derived from consumption depends both on the absolute level of consumption and the level of current consumption relative to past consumption – that households do not like consuming less than they have been and initially resist changes, before eventually adjusting. This modification was shown to substantially improve the fit of the model.10

Other work, predominantly concerned with why the behaviour of consumption appears to invalidate the permanent income hypothesis, such as Muellbauer (1988), suggests that households may be myopic in their consumption choices. Campbell and Mankiw (1989) offer the hypothesis that households do not have the resources to engage in producing full forecasts and so it is optimal for them to use a rule of thumb when updating their consumption plans in response to income shocks.

That lagged output improves the fit with the data is important, but whether one accepts the habit formation story, the rule of thumb hypothesis or simply assumes that households are less forward-looking than is often suggested, is less important for the specification of the IS relation. In empirical work, an assumption of habit formation or myopia in household consumption choices is not uncommon and both Batini and Haldane (1999) and Smets and Wooters (2003) allow for it in their respective models of the UK and the euro area economies. Indeed, neither Batini and Haldane nor Carlin and Soskice (2010) include expected output in their baseline IS relations, an approach which I follow here. Therefore, the baseline IS relation employed here includes lags of output, real interest rates and exchange rates but not expectations thereof:
\[ y_{t} = \beta_y y_{t-j|t} + \beta_r z_{t-j|t} + \beta_{er} er_{t-j} + \mu_{t} \]

13.4 Empirical model specification

We amend our theoretical equation in a likewise fashion:

\[y_t = \beta_y y_{t-j|t} - \rho(i_t - \pi_{t-j|t} - r^*) + \beta_{er} er_{t-j|t} + \mu_t\]

THis leads to our expirical specification. All variables (unless otherwise noted) are expressed as log-deviations from their steady-state trends. Aggregate demand depends on expected future output, real interest rates, and the real exchange rate (the expenditure-switching effect):

\(\text{HOG IS Curve 1:} \quad y_t = \beta_1 y_{t-1} + \beta_2 y_{t+1|t} + \beta_3 r_t + \beta_4 z_t + \beta_5 (\mathbb{1}_{ZLB} \cdot z_t) + \beta_6 x_t + \beta_7 er_{t-1} + \mathbf{D}\gamma_{IS} + \epsilon_{y,t}\)

\(y_{t}\) is the output gap, or the percentage deviation of real (chained volumes measure of) GDP at market prices from its long-term cyclical trend4.

\(y_{t-1}\) is the lagged output gap (reflecting output trend persistence) and \(y_{t+1|t}\) is a lead output gap (to take account of future output expectations - single-lead expectations, \(t+1\)).

\(r_t\)is the real interest rate gap \(R_{GAP} = r^{base} - r^{*} = i_t - \pi_{t+j|t} - r^*\). We start with the official real bank rate, which is the nominal BOE base rate minus annualised quarterly inflation [log(CPI) / lag(CPI)]. We adopted the average of OBR’s annual forecast inflation for the period 2026-2029 as our proxy for inflation expectations, leading to as our assumption for the neutral rate (\(r^*\)) and subtracted that from the real bank rate. This real interest rate gap represents the monetary policy stance; a positive value indicating restrictive policy (leaning against the wind), while a negative value indicates accommodative policy.

\(z\) is the spread on the nominal par value of 5-year gilt yields over and above (or below) the nominal base rate (so \(i^{mkt} = i^{base} + z\)), which we include in the analysis to account for the zero lower bound issue.

\(\mathbb{1}_{ZLB}\) is a binary dummy variable equal to one when the bank rate is at its effective lower bound (0.1-0.5) and zero otherwise. This makes \(\beta_5\) an interaction coefficient - the additional impact of the market spread on the output gap when conventional policy is constrained.5

\(x_{t}\) is the change in net exports or, more precisely, the difference between the log-differenced CVMs of exports and imports at market prices. Net export growth can be volatile, so accounting for the contribution of each to the overall change before combining them helps smooth the data.

\(er_{t-1}\) is the lagged real exchange rate gap. In an open-economy IS curve, the Real Exchange Rate (RER) gap represents the “expenditure-switching” effect. When the pound is “cheap” (a negative gap), it should eventually boost the output gap (y) by making UK exports more competitive. The lag is important because it is empirically more robust than the spot rate, as it takes 2-4 quarters for trade flows to respond to price changes.6

\(\mathbf{D} \cdot \gamma_{IS}\) represents a set of pulse dummy variables for the Covid-19 shock.

\(\epsilon_{y,t}\) is the model’s error term.

Sign Expectations: Note that while \(\beta_3\) (real rate) and \(\beta_4\) (spread) should be negative (tighter conditions = lower output), \(\beta_6\) (real exchange rate) should be positive if an increase in the index represents a depreciation (making exports cheaper).Given the results, we refined to a tidier model

\[ \text{HOG IS Curve 2:} \quad y_t = \beta_1 y_{t-1} + \beta_2 y_{t+1|t} + \beta_3 r_t + \beta_4 er_{t-1} + \mathbf{D}\gamma_{IS} + \epsilon_{y,t} \]

\(y_{t}\): the current output gap.

\(y_{t-1}\) and \(y_{t+1|t}\): lagged and lead output gaps (persistence and expectations).

\(r_t\): the real interest rate gap, as already defined

\(er_{t-1}\): the lagged real exchange rate gap, reflecting the time delay in the expenditure switching effect.

\(\mathbf{D} \cdot \gamma_{IS}\): COVID pulse dummies.This removes non-significant terms and produces a better, more parsimonious result, for the following reasons:

  • Increased Precision (Lower Standard Errors): on the output and interest rate gaps.

  • Higher Adjusted R-squared: While the Multiple R-squared dropped slightly (0.9482 to 0.947), the Adjusted R-squared actually went UP (0.9435 to 0.9439). This suggests that the variables we removed weren’t adding enough explanatory power to justify their inclusion.

  • Stronger Significance for Exchange Rates: The lagged real exchange rate gap p-value improved (from 0.0186 to 0.0136) and suggests that removing the net exports term permits the impact of the real exchange rate to be seen more clearly. This suggests the price effect (exchange rate) is a more reliable lead indicator for UK aggregate demand than the volatile volume data (net exports). This makes the argument for the “expenditure-switching” effect in the UK much more statistically robust.

  • Leaner: it retains the “hybrid” structure (lag and lead output gaps) and the essential policy tools (Interest rate gap and exchange rate gap). This matches the core of the OBR Murray (2012) logic without the empirical “clutter.”

What the model suggests is that the UK output gap is driven by strong internal momentum (0.74), forward-looking expectations (0.17), a sensitive response to real interest rate deviations (-0.04), and a delayed boost from currency depreciation (0.03)7.
